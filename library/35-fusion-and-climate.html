Project Lux · The question fusion is usually sold on, and the answer the models actually give

1. The question, asked properly#

Fusion is routinely presented as a climate solution. It is worth separating two claims that get conflated:

  1. Fusion is a low-carbon energy source. True, and well supported (Document 33).
  2. Fusion will help meet 2050 climate targets. This is a different claim, it is testable against integrated assessment models, and the answer is largely no.

The distinction matters because it determines what fusion should be funded for, and by whom.

2. What the integrated assessment models say#

Integrated assessment models — GCAM, TIMES, MESSAGE and their relatives — simulate the global energy system under cost minimisation subject to a carbon constraint. They answer: if the world minimises the cost of decarbonising, when and how much does it buy of each technology?

The MIT / GCAM result#

The most cited analysis examined what cost levels fusion must reach by 2050 to achieve strong market penetration and contribute to decarbonising global electricity in the second half of the century.

Fusion cost trajectory Reduction in global decarbonisation cost
$8,000/kW in 2050 → $4,300/kW in 2100 $3.6 trillion
$5,600/kW in 2050 → $3,000/kW in 2100 $8.7 trillion

And on market share: in a deep-decarbonisation scenario, the global share of electricity generation from fusion in 2100 ranges from under 10% if costs are high to more than 50% if costs are low.

Those are enormous numbers, and they are the basis of most "fusion is worth trillions" claims. Note carefully what they are: 2100 figures, contingent on hitting cost targets that Document 10 shows are at the optimistic end of a thirty-fold uncertainty range.

The finding that is usually left out#

From the same body of work, and considerably more sobering:

In analysed scenarios with "fast track" deployment, fusion becomes available from 2050 — but total system cost minimisation does not include fusion before 2070 in any case, due to high initial investment costs.

Read that again. Even in a scenario where fusion is available in 2050, a cost-minimising model does not choose to build it until around 2070, because in 2050 something else is cheaper.

That something else is solar, wind and storage, which by 2050 will have had another twenty-five years on a learning curve fusion has not yet started.

The regional split#

The models produce a clear geographic pattern:

Europe and America do not need nuclear fusion for a transition to clean energy by 2050, while Asian regions with large industrial development relying on fossil fuels may require fusion as part of decarbonisation alternatives.

The logic is straightforward. Regions with good renewable resources, declining demand growth, and land available for wind and solar can decarbonise without fusion. Regions with high population density, rapid industrial demand growth, poor renewable resource per capita, and heavy industry requiring high-temperature heat cannot easily — and those are precisely the places where firm, land-light, siteable-anywhere generation is worth a premium.

This has an uncomfortable implication for how fusion is funded. The countries spending the most on fusion — the US, UK, Germany, EU — are, per the models, the countries that need it least for their own 2050 targets. The regions that would benefit most are in Asia, and China is the one already investing at scale.

3. The IEA's 2026 framing#

The IEA published The State of Energy Innovation 2026 on 17 February 2026, its annual assessment for policymakers ahead of the IEA Ministerial and Energy Innovation Forum.

Its treatment of fusion is notable for placing it on equal footing with other emerging technologies rather than as a special case — assessed alongside batteries, geothermal and solar on status, milestones and challenges.

The milestone the IEA identifies for fusion: "First fusion plant to demonstrate the technical viability of producing saleable energy."

That phrasing is precise and worth noting. Not net energy. Not Ignition. Not Q>1. Saleable energy — the IEA's chosen marker is commercial, not scientific.

The IEA's stated obstacle:

Despite recent attention to engineering the first integrated fusion energy plants, the fuel cycle and materials are still not ready for scale-up. Fusion reactors expose components to extreme neutron fluxes that gradually degrade structural integrity... overcoming these challenges will require expanded access to testing facilities and sustained long-term funding.

This is the same conclusion Document 09 reaches from the technical literature and Document 07 reaches from the companies' own survey responses. Three independent sources — the IEA, the DOE roadmap, and the industry itself — now agree that materials and the fuel cycle are the binding constraints, and that the missing input is test facilities.

The IEA also notes the strategic shift: "a race to develop commercial fusion energy is spurring new strategies and funding," and that while public support for international collaboration continues, recent initiatives reflect a shift towards domestic commercialisation — which is exactly the pattern documented across Document 08.

4. So what is fusion actually for?#

If it does not help meet 2050 targets, the honest case has to be made on different grounds. There are four, and they are all reasonable.

4.1 The second half of the century is not optional#

Net zero by 2050 is not the end of the problem. Global electricity demand is projected to grow for decades after, driven by electrification of transport, heat and industry, by AI compute (Document 10), and by development in the countries that use least today. A technology that supplies 50% of world electricity in 2100 is not a failure for having missed 2050.

4.2 Renewables have a hard fraction#

Solar and wind decarbonise electricity cheaply up to a high penetration. The last 10–20% — firm capacity through long low-wind, low-sun periods; high-temperature industrial heat; systems where land or transmission is the binding constraint — is disproportionately expensive. Fusion's value is concentrated in exactly that fraction, which is why its realistic LCOE of $110–145/MWh is not disqualifying: it competes against the marginal cost of the last increment, not the average.

4.3 The insurance argument#

Every 1.5 °C pathway depends on assumptions — about storage cost curves, about CCS deployment, about public acceptance of transmission and land use, about fission's political trajectory — any of which could fail. Fusion is a hedge against those pathways not working, and hedges are worth paying for even when the base case looks fine. At roughly $14 billion of cumulative private investment, it is a cheap hedge relative to the trillions at stake.

4.4 It is not only about climate any more#

This is the change since 2023 and it is documented throughout this dossier. The FIA's own framing: environmental threats and energy security "remain, but the last year has added an important new driver: the energy demand of artificial intelligence."

Fusion's current funding surge is driven by hyperscalers who want firm, siteable, carbon-free power near their load and are willing to pay a premium for it. That is an energy-security and industrial-competitiveness argument as much as a climate one, and it is a more robust basis for a fifteen-year capital programme than a climate target that fusion will miss anyway.

5. The uncomfortable framings, stated fairly#

"Fusion is a distraction from deploying what works." The strongest version of the critique: money and political attention spent on a technology that arrives after 2070 could deploy solar, wind, storage and transmission now, when the emissions actually matter. There is genuine merit here, and the honest response is about magnitude — global fusion investment, public and private, is a small fraction of one year's clean energy capital expenditure, and the funding sources are largely not fungible with deployment budgets. Venture capital and hyperscaler balance sheets do not otherwise buy transmission lines.

"Fusion is being sold on a climate case it cannot meet." Substantially fair as a criticism of fusion communication, if not of fusion. The IAM results above are not obscure, and they rarely appear in industry material.

"By 2070 something else will have solved it." Possibly. Cheap long-duration storage, or advanced geothermal, or fission finally achieving a learning curve, would each reduce fusion's addressable market considerably. This is a real risk to the investment case and is not a reason to stop.

6. Assessment#

On the evidence, fusion is not a 2050 climate technology. The models are consistent: it is not available in time, and even where it is available it is not chosen, because the alternatives are cheaper for the specific job of decarbonising electricity by mid-century.

Fusion is a second-half-of-the-century energy technology, whose value is concentrated in the hard fraction that renewables serve badly, in regions that cannot decarbonise on renewables alone, and in applications — hyperscale compute, industrial heat, isolated systems, marine and space — where firmness and siteability outweigh price.

The cost target is the whole question. At $5,600/kW in 2050 falling to $3,000/kW by 2100, fusion takes over half of world electricity generation and saves $8.7 trillion. At the top of the $1,400–43,000/kW range, it takes under 10% and remains a niche. Everything in Document 09, Document 24 and Document 30 — materials lifetime, availability, remote maintenance, design margin — feeds into which of those two worlds happens.

The most defensible public case for funding fusion is therefore not "it will save the climate." It is: this is a cheap hedge against the second half of the century, in a category renewables serve badly, with an identified near-term customer already paying a premium, in a strategic industry the alternative is ceding to China. That case is stronger and considerably more honest than the one usually made.
