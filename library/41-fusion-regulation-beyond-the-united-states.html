Project Lux · Different institutions, converging hazards, and the distance between a policy decision and a licensable plant

1. There is no single international fusion regime#

The phrase “fusion regulation” suggests a choice between regulating fusion like fission and exempting it as harmless. Actual systems are more granular. Jurisdictions allocate radiation protection, worker safety, environmental assessment, nuclear security, safeguards, waste, planning and emergency functions to different institutions. The same facility can be outside a nuclear-installation licensing statute while remaining tightly governed under other law.

The United Kingdom and China now provide especially useful contrasts. The UK has deliberately excluded fusion facilities from its fission-oriented nuclear-site licensing regime and retained environmental and occupational regulators. China has placed fusion devices into three categories under a centrally administered radiation-safety licensing framework. Both are adopted policy choices. Neither means that every commercial design standard or permitting question is complete.

This document complements the standards and liability analysis in Document 23. Its organising question is not “which country is easiest?” but “which hazards are governed, by whom, at what stage, and with what evidence?”

2. The regulatory object is a system, not a reaction#

The deuterium–tritium reaction is not a self-propagating fission chain reaction. Loss of confinement ends fusion power rapidly. That supports proportionate treatment, but it does not eliminate hazards. A plant may contain large tritium inventories, activated structures, high magnetic and cryogenic energies, hazardous coolants, beryllium or lithium, intense neutron fields and industrial turbines. Document 33 separates intrinsic plasma shutdown from the engineered safety of the facility around it.

Regulators therefore need a graded inventory of functions:

  • radiological protection for workers and the public;
  • confinement and accountancy of tritium;
  • activation-product and waste management;
  • environmental assessment and routine discharge control;
  • industrial, pressure, electrical, magnetic and cryogenic safety;
  • security, safeguards and export controls where applicable;
  • land-use planning, construction and grid permission;
  • decommissioning finance and end-state obligations.

Whether those functions sit in one “nuclear” licence or several existing regimes is an institutional design choice. A lighter central licence can still produce a demanding total pathway. Conversely, labelling a facility nuclear does not guarantee that its fusion-specific evidence standards are mature.

3. United Kingdom: function-based regulation#

The Energy Act 2023 established the UK’s central policy decision: fusion energy facilities are excluded from nuclear-site licensing. The UK Fusion Strategy, published 16 March 2026, confirms that the Environment Agency and Health and Safety Executive—or devolved equivalents—remain the principal environmental and workplace-safety authorities rather than the Office for Nuclear Regulation’s nuclear-site regime.

The adopted component is the institutional route. The government’s strategy describes it as proportionate and technology-appropriate, but also states that security and other residual positions require further clarification during the current Parliament. A complete commercial framework is therefore partly designed or announced as of 26 August 2026. It would be inaccurate either to say that fusion is unregulated or that every rule is settled.

The UK approach has three advantages. Existing regulators already understand industrial hazards; requirements can be scaled to inventories and credible releases; and early clarity can support the STEP programme and private developers. Its challenge is coordination. A developer may need environmental permits, worker-safety compliance, planning consent, tritium controls and security decisions from different bodies. The absence of a nuclear-site licence removes one architecture, not the need for a coherent assurance case.

STEP is also a government programme and planned prototype, not proof that the regime has licensed an operating fusion power station. Regulatory learning will occur alongside design, site development and supply-chain qualification.

4. China: classified devices under central radiation licensing#

China’s Ministry of Ecology and Environment issued its fusion-device radiation-safety notice on 2 April 2025 and published it on 7 April 2025. It replaces a 2016 magnetic-confinement instruction and divides the field into:

  1. plasma-physics experimental devices;
  2. deuterium–tritium fusion experimental devices; and
  3. fusion-energy application devices.

The Ministry supervises these by reference to nuclear-technology-use facilities and issues radiation-safety licences. Regional nuclear and radiation safety offices perform routine inspection. Environmental-impact assessment and radiation licensing are linked, with a combined review to be explored; existing approvals remain valid.

This is a materially more specific framework than saying that China “supports fusion”. Classification allows requirements to grow with fuel, inventory and application. A non-D–T experiment is not automatically treated like a D–T device, and an application device is distinguished from an experiment.

The notice does not, by itself, settle every rule for a future power plant. It leaves room for detailed standards, security, liability, waste and decommissioning provisions to develop. Nor does a central licence prove that private programmes such as ENN, Startorus or YAN Fusion have met commercial-plant requirements. It establishes an adopted administrative pathway against which such projects can be evaluated.

5. European Union: layered authority and an announced strategy#

The European Union adds another structural problem: competencies are split between EU law, Euratom institutions and national licensing authorities. Radiation protection, safeguards, environmental assessment, research policy and market rules operate at different levels, while a facility is licensed in a member state. France and Germany therefore share parts of a legal environment without possessing one identical fusion licensing process.

As of the Round 4 search on 26 August 2026, the European Commission’s first Fusion Strategy was still described as planned for 2026; Project Lux did not verify an adopted strategy. Document 08 must preserve that as announced, not completed. A strategy, when adopted, may coordinate research, supply chains, skills and regulation, but it will not automatically replace national facility permission.

The practical consequence for developers is early jurisdiction mapping. A demonstration may need to show compliance with common radiation and environmental principles while negotiating a national category that was written for research reactors, accelerators or industrial radiation facilities. Regulatory convergence will more likely begin with shared technical standards and safety cases than with one supranational licence.

6. Japan: industrial strategy ahead of a complete licence#

Japan’s Seventh Strategic Energy Plan and 2026 policy materials support earlier fusion realisation, industrial participation and multiple confinement approaches. They also call for scientifically rational safety study and international coordination. That is a policy direction, not evidence of a complete commercial licensing regime.

Japan illustrates why strategy and regulation should be recorded separately. Government endorsement can fund facilities, coordinate industry and set milestones while the legal classification and detailed evidence requirements remain under development. A future device’s fuel, neutron output and inventory may determine which existing laws apply and what new provisions are needed.

The honest status as of 26 August 2026 is therefore framework in development. It should not be reported as regulatory absence, because ministries and safety work exist; nor as completed licensing readiness, because Project Lux has not captured a comprehensive adopted commercial rule set.

7. International Atomic Energy Agency: convergence without a world licence#

The IAEA can convene regulators, develop terminology, publish safety guidance and support safeguards discussion. It cannot issue the construction or operating licence for a national project. Its value is convergence: comparable hazard categories, shared operational experience, consistent waste characterisation and common expectations for safety assessment.

Fusion needs that convergence because machines and suppliers are international. A magnet, tritium system or remote-maintenance package qualified in one programme will be more valuable if another regulator can understand the evidence. Without common language, each project must translate its safety case from first principles.

International coordination also preserves two distinctions. First, fusion’s inability to sustain a runaway chain reaction does not erase radiological or industrial hazards. Second, applying proportionate rules does not imply identical rules for every concept. A low-neutron fuel-cycle proposal, a pulsed deuterium–tritium machine and a large steady-state plant should not inherit one undifferentiated category merely because all produce fusion reactions.

8. Comparing the architectures#

The UK asks existing environmental and worker-safety institutions to regulate fusion functions outside nuclear-site licensing. China classifies devices and uses central radiation-safety licensing with regional inspection. EU member states operate inside layered European and national obligations. Japan is aligning industrial acceleration with an emerging safety framework.

These arrangements differ in institutional form but face common evidence questions:

  • What is the maximum mobilisable tritium and activated-material inventory?
  • Which accident sequences produce credible releases, and how are they bounded?
  • What passive and active barriers remain after loss of power or coolant?
  • How will waste be characterised, stored and cleared or disposed?
  • Which software and control functions are safety-significant?
  • Who pays for decommissioning if the operator fails?
  • How are design changes and learning from experiments fed back into permissions?

Those questions are more durable than labels. They also create a route to mutual learning: regulators can compare answers even when statutes differ.

9. What “proportionate” should mean#

Proportionate regulation is often used as a synonym for faster regulation. It should instead mean that requirements scale with credible hazards, uncertainty and consequence. Early experimental devices may justify bounded permissions and staged hold points. A commercial plant with a large tritium throughput and long operating mission should carry a stronger case even if the plasma remains intrinsically self-terminating.

Staging can reduce both over- and under-regulation. A regulator can authorise construction, non-nuclear commissioning, low-inventory operation and later fuel phases separately. The developer gains earlier feedback; the regulator avoids pretending that unbuilt systems have already been demonstrated. Conditions can be tied to measured leakage, material activation, control performance and emergency exercises.

The same principle applies to announced designs. Company schedules are projections. Government strategies are commitments. Experimental results are demonstrations. A licensing framework is adopted when the responsible authority has actually established it; a plant is licensable only when a concrete design supplies the required evidence.

10. The decisive metric is completeness, not branding#

As of 26 August 2026, the UK and China have made clear but different institutional choices. The EU strategy remains announced in the evidence reviewed here, and Japan’s comprehensive commercial route remains in development. No jurisdiction examined has demonstrated routine licensing and oversight of an operating commercial fusion power plant, because no such plant exists.

The most credible path is not global uniformity. It is interoperable assurance: common hazard language, traceable evidence and staged permissions that national institutions can apply proportionately. Regulatory advantage will belong to jurisdictions that make responsibilities legible early, close residual gaps without changing categories unpredictably, and demand stronger evidence as experiments become fuel-cycle facilities and facilities become power plants.

Waste classification divergence#

The regulatory divergence documented here extends to waste. Fusion Waste: Classification, Clearance and Disposal sets out how UK and French classification schemes assign the same fusion component to different classes, and therefore to different disposal routes and different regulators. Divergence in licensing is compounded by divergence in disposal.
