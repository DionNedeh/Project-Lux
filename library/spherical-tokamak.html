A Tokamak squashed toward a cored apple: the major radius is pulled in until the aspect ratio A = R₀/a approaches unity. The physics case is beta — a low-aspect-ratio plasma holds more pressure for a given field, and fusion power density scales as β²B⁴.

The vault's spherical machines are ST40, MAST-U, Globus-M2, and the two power-plant designs STEP and ST-E1.

What the geometry costs#

Everything that has to fit around the plasma now has to fit through the centre column, and that single constraint propagates through the whole design. ST-E1's neutronics paper is the clearest evidence this vault holds for how hard it bites:

  • Inboard breeding is ruled out entirely for lack of space, so all breeding is outboard — and the outboard vertical shield has to reflect neutrons back into the blanket to recover TBR.
  • The centre column carries the machine's worst radiation damage: up to ≈ 50 dpa per full-power year in the tungsten carbide shielding block at a thin part of the midplane.
  • Shielding the HTS magnets "is a major driver dictating tokamak size, plant lifetime, and balance of plant" — a notable ordering, in which the magnets, not the plasma, size the machine.

Warning

The shielding problem is not solved in the design this vault holds best ST-E1's baseline exceeds its own HTS fast-flux limit by a factor of about 2.4, giving an estimated 13 full-power years against a 30 fpy design intent. A larger 2400 MW variant at aspect ratio 2.31 recovers roughly 25 fpy. See ST-E1.

Why it attracts private capital anyway#

A compact machine is cheaper to build and faster to iterate, which is why Tokamak Energy and the UK's STEP programme both took this route, and why UKAEA's materials strategy names the spherical tokamak with a liquid-metal breeder blanket as its target asset. The trade is explicit: accept the hardest possible inboard engineering problem in exchange for size.

Compare Magnetic Confinement Devices for the conventional-aspect-ratio alternative, and Power Plant Designs Compared for how the designs line up.
