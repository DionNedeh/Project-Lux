Project Lux · The largest scientific collaboration in history, its 2024 reckoning, and what it will actually prove

1. What ITER is#

ITER — originally the International Thermonuclear Experimental Reactor, now officially just "ITER", and glossed as Latin for "the way" — is a Tokamak under construction at Saint-Paul-lès-Durance in Provence, France. It is designed to produce 500 MW of fusion power from 50 MW of input heating power (Q = 10) for 400-second pulses, and to sustain Q ≥ 5 for 1,000 seconds or more in long-pulse operation.

It is not a power plant. It will generate no electricity. Its purpose is to demonstrate, at a scale unambiguously relevant to a reactor, that a burning plasma — one heated predominantly by its own fusion products — can be created and controlled, and to test the nuclear technologies a power plant would need.

Machine parameters#

Parameter Value
Major radius R 6.2 m
Minor radius a 2.0 m
Plasma volume 840 m³
Toroidal field on axis 5.3 T
Plasma current 15 MA
Fusion power 500 MW
Q (inductive) 10 for 400 s
Q (long pulse) ≥5 for 1,000+ s
Total machine mass ~23,000 tonnes
Cryostat 30 m diameter × 30 m tall, ~3,850 t — the largest stainless steel vacuum chamber ever built
Superconductor Nb₃Sn (TF and CS), NbTi (PF and correction coils)
Operating temperature 4.5 K
Superconducting strand ~100,000 km of Nb₃Sn — enough to circle the Earth 2.5 times

The magnet system is the defining component: 18 toroidal field coils, a central solenoid of six stacked modules weighing about 1,000 tonnes and generating 13 T, 6 poloidal field coils, and 18 correction coils. It stores roughly 51 gigajoules of magnetic energy and is the largest and most powerful pulsed superconducting magnet system ever built.

2. Governance and money#

The Members#

Seven Members representing 35 nations: the European Union (through Fusion for Energy), China, India, Japan, South Korea, Russia, and the United States.

Cost sharing for construction:

  • European Union: ~45.5% (as host)
  • China, India, Japan, Korea, Russia, USA: ~9.1% each

The in-kind model — ITER's structural flaw#

Roughly 90% of Member contributions are delivered in kind: not money, but completed components manufactured in the Member's own territory, in its own currency, by its own industry.

The rationale was political and industrial: every Member gets domestic jobs, domestic supply-chain capability, and domestic technology transfer, which is what makes a 35-nation project fundable at all. Russia, for instance, manufactures and supplies 25 distinct high-technology systems; the United States supplied the entire central solenoid.

The cost is enormous complexity. A single component may require first-of-a-kind manufacturing in a country with no prior experience of it, to tolerances no one has met before, with the ITER Organization holding design authority but not contractual control over the supplier. Every delay in one Member's factory propagates into an assembly sequence that has almost no float. Physics Today's assessment of the arrangement is that it makes a precise overall cost estimate impossible in principle, because the contributions are denominated in different currencies, labour markets and accounting conventions.

What it costs#

There is no single agreed number, and this is a genuine feature of the structure rather than evasion.

  • ITER Organization framing: roughly €18–22 billion to reach first plasma, based on the EU's €6.6 bn estimate for its contributions plus the IO's €6.9 bn for its own operations.
  • Extrapolating the US cost estimate across all Members yields about $49.5 billion (€44 bn) — a figure the source itself describes as "highly inaccurate", largely because US procurement and contracting costs are far higher than elsewhere.
  • The US Congressional Research Service and the GAO have both examined the US share, which has grown substantially from original estimates.

A defensible statement: ITER is a project in the tens of billions of dollars, most plausibly in the €20–45 bn range depending entirely on accounting convention, making it comparable to the International Space Station and one of the most expensive scientific instruments ever constructed.

3. The 2024 rebaseline — the most important thing to understand about ITER#

What was wrong#

The previous baseline was built around a staged start: achieve "First Plasma" in December 2025 with a deliberately incomplete machine — no heating systems of consequence, incomplete in-vessel components, minimal diagnostics — and then progressively install the rest during a series of shutdowns.

Two things broke it:

  1. Technical non-conformities. In 2022, defects were found in two critical items: the vacuum vessel sector welds (dimensional non-conformities in the field joints) and corrosion in the thermal shield cooling pipes. Both required repair campaigns of years, not months, on components already delivered.
  2. The staged approach was itself a mistake. Repeatedly disassembling and reassembling a machine of this complexity to install missing systems turned out, on review, to cost more time than building it complete in the first place.

What replaced it#

At the 34th ITER Council on 19–20 June 2024, the Council endorsed a revised baseline built on a different philosophy: start later, but start with a substantially complete machine.

Milestone Old baseline Baseline 2024
First Plasma 2025 concept abandoned
Start of Research Operation (SRO) — 2034
Deuterium-deuterium operation ~2035 2035
Full magnetic energy 2033 2036
Start of Deuterium-Tritium Operation 2035 2039

SRO in 2034 is followed by 27 months of substantive research using hydrogen and deuterium-deuterium plasmas, building up to long pulses at full magnetic energy and full plasma current.

How to judge it#

The delay of the D-T phase to 2039 — four years — was a serious blow, and it is the principal reason the private sector's confidence that it can move faster is now widely shared. But the new baseline is generally regarded within the field as technically better sequenced and more honest than what it replaced. It eliminates the disassembly cycles, front-loads risk reduction through the Magnet Cold Test Facility and global commissioning, and delivers a machine capable of doing real science from its first campaign.

The ITER Council's June 2026 meeting reported the project as on budget and on schedule against Baseline 2024 — the first time in many years such a statement has been credible.

4. Status, August 2026#

Magnets — complete#

All components of the magnet system have been manufactured. The final piece was the sixth module of the central solenoid, built and tested in the United States; US ITER completed its final central-solenoid deliveries in March 2026. Module stacking, followed by application of a compression structure to pre-load the stack, proceeds through 2026.

This is a genuine landmark: the world's largest and most powerful pulsed superconducting magnet system is finished, and its manufacture built a global Nb₃Sn supply chain that did not previously exist. Production of Nb₃Sn strand for ITER increased world capacity by roughly an order of magnitude.

Assembly — in progress#

  • Two completed sector modules left the assembly tooling and were installed in the tokamak pit in April and June 2026, with a third following. Each module combines a 440-tonne vacuum vessel sector with two toroidal field coils and thermal shielding, weighing well over 1,000 tonnes as an assembly.
  • The vacuum vessel sector weld and thermal shield repairs are complete.
  • The Magnet Cold Test Facility has begun operation — testing coils at 4.5 K before installation, catching problems while they are still fixable.
  • Feeder assembly reached a first-phase milestone.
  • Plant systems installation and site works continue.

Licensing — a quiet but significant win#

France's nuclear safety authority agreed to exclude the ITER vacuum vessel from the scope of French legislation implementing the European Pressure Equipment Directive. This sounds procedural; it is not. The PED imposes a conformity regime designed for industrial pressure vessels that maps poorly onto a one-off nuclear fusion component, and compliance had been a material schedule and cost risk. Removing it is expected to improve both.

ITER remains a licensed French nuclear facility (Installation Nucléaire de Base, licensed November 2012) — the first fusion device in the world to be licensed as a nuclear installation, which makes its licensing file a reference document for every fusion regulator since.

5. What ITER will actually prove — and what it will not#

It will prove#

1. Burning plasma physics. This is the core deliverable and nothing else can substitute for it. At Q = 10, alpha particles supply about two-thirds of the plasma heating. That creates a strongly coupled, self-regulating, nonlinear system that has never existed in a magnetic device. Specific unknowns ITER will address:

  • Whether alpha-driven Alfvén eigenmodes transport the alphas out before they thermalise
  • Whether burn control is stable — can you throttle a burning plasma, or does it run away or Quench?
  • Whether the confinement scaling laws extrapolated from present machines actually hold at reactor scale

2. Integrated operation at scale. Superconducting magnets, cryogenics, tritium plant, remote handling, heating systems, and a tungsten Divertor all operating together on one machine, at reactor scale, for the first time.

3. Tritium breeding — as a test. ITER will be the first device ever to test tritium breeding blanket modules, via the Test Blanket Module program: six different TBM concepts from different Members installed in equatorial ports. This is a small-scale test of a technology on which every fusion power plant depends absolutely, and it is arguably ITER's highest-value nuclear deliverable.

4. Materials under real fusion neutron flux — at low dose. ITER will accumulate under 2 dpa over its lifetime.

5. Licensing precedent. ITER's safety case, licensed under French nuclear law, is the reference every subsequent regulator consults.

It will not prove#

  • Net electricity. ITER produces no power output. Q = 10 is scientific gain; ITER's own wall-plug consumption during a pulse is several hundred megawatts.
  • Tritium self-sufficiency. The TBM program tests modules, not a closed fuel cycle. ITER runs on externally supplied tritium — and will consume a meaningful fraction of the world's civilian inventory doing so.
  • Materials for a power plant. 2 dpa versus 15 dpa per year. ITER cannot answer the materials question and was never designed to; that is what IFMIF-DONES and FPNS are for.
  • Economic viability. Nothing about ITER's cost structure says anything about a commercial plant's.
  • Steady state. 400-second pulses, extending to 1,000+ seconds at lower Q. Not continuous operation.

6. The criticisms, taken seriously#

ITER attracts more criticism than any other project in physics. The substantive versions are these.

"It is too slow to matter." SPARC intends to demonstrate Q > 1 in 2027 — twelve years before ITER runs D-T — at perhaps 1% of the cost. If the compact high-field route works, ITER will be superseded on its central scientific claim before it operates. Counter: SPARC targets Q > 2 in short pulses at 140 MW; ITER targets Q = 10 at 500 MW for 400 s with a full nuclear technology suite. These are not the same experiment, and the burning-plasma physics ITER will study at 840 m³ has no substitute. Counter-counter: if SPARC gets there first and cheaply, the marginal scientific value of ITER's result falls considerably, whatever its rigour.

"The in-kind model is structurally dysfunctional." This is largely conceded, including by ITER's own management. It is the price of the diplomacy that makes the project possible.

"It consumed the field's resources for thirty years." This is the most serious charge. Through the 1990s and 2000s, ITER's cost absorbed the fusion budgets of most participating nations, and the intermediate machines that would have continued the six-orders-of-magnitude climb in triple product were not built. Magnetic fusion's performance largely plateaued from about 1997 to 2021 — not because the physics stalled, but because no new machines were commissioned. Counter: without ITER as a unifying goal, national fusion budgets would very likely have been cut rather than redirected.

"It will be obsolete on arrival." By 2039, HTS magnets, AI plasma control, and additive manufacturing will have advanced far beyond what ITER's 1990s-2000s design assumes. Counter: ITER's scientific mission does not depend on its technology being current; it depends on its plasma being large and hot enough, which it will be.

The fair verdict#

ITER is simultaneously the most important physics experiment in the field and a cautionary tale in scientific megaproject management. Both are true. The burning-plasma data it produces will be irreplaceable and will inform every reactor design for a century. The path by which it will produce that data cost roughly two decades more than it should have, and the private sector's entire existence is, in part, a response to that fact.

The healthiest reading of the current landscape is that ITER and the private sector are no longer competitors but complements on different timescales: the companies will attempt fast, cheap, high-risk demonstrations in the late 2020s and 2030s, and ITER will supply the deep, rigorous, reactor-scale physics that no privately financed machine will ever be able to justify.

7. ITER's satellites#

The ITER agreement spawned a family of associated programs, several of which now matter independently:

  • JT-60SA (Japan/EU) — built under the Broader Approach as compensation for Japan losing the site bid. Now the largest operating superconducting tokamak, exploring ITER- and DEMO-relevant scenarios ahead of ITER. Next campaign end-2026.
  • IFMIF/EVEDA → IFMIF-DONES (Granada, Spain) — the engineering validation program for the fusion materials irradiation facility, now the leading candidate to close the materials gap ITER cannot address.
  • The Broader Approach — also includes the International Fusion Energy Research Centre and remote experimentation capabilities in Japan.
  • Fusion for Energy (F4E) — the EU's domestic agency, and by extension the organisation that has built European fusion industrial capability.
  • US ITER (managed by Oak Ridge National Laboratory) — which delivered the central solenoid and, in the process, built US capability in large superconducting magnets that now benefits the domestic private sector directly.

That last point deserves emphasis: ITER's most durable legacy may turn out to be industrial rather than scientific. The Nb₃Sn supply chain, the large-scale cryogenics capability, the precision manufacturing base, and the trained workforce all exist because ITER paid for them, and all of them now serve companies that will reach a plant before ITER reaches D-T.
