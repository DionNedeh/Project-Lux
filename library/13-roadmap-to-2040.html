Project Lux · What is scheduled, what is plausible, and what is marketing

How to read this document#

Fusion timelines have a poor historical record. This document therefore separates three categories, and labels every entry:

  • 🟩 Scheduled — funded, contracted, under construction, or committed by a government. High confidence it happens, though not necessarily on time.
  • 🟨 Projected — a credible organisation's stated plan, with hardware in progress but material technical risk remaining.
  • 🟥 Aspirational — a stated goal without the intervening steps demonstrated. Directionally informative, not a forecast.

Slippage in fusion has historically run at roughly one year per year for programs without dedicated funding, and roughly 20–50% schedule growth for funded first-of-a-kind hardware. Apply that prior generously.

Phase 1: 2026–2028 — The proof phase#

The question of this phase: does a magnetically confined plasma produce net energy, and can anything put fusion electricity on a wire?

2026 (remainder)#

Event
🟩 NRC final fusion rule — byproduct-material framework, targeted October 2026
🟩 SPARC magnet installation completes — all 18 TF coils in place by end of summer
🟩 ITER central solenoid stacking and continued sector module installation
🟩 JT-60SA restart — new campaign begins end-2026, running ~6 months
🟩 EU Fusion Strategy published — the bloc's first
🟩 Germany's FIRE roadmap (Fusion Energy Research and Innovation) due end-2026
🟩 German fusion hubs VEGA, STRIDE, MAT-TRIX begin operating (€125 m initial)
🟨 Astral Systems' Berkeley high-flux DT irradiation facility opens Q4 2026
🟨 Continued NIF Ignition shots; possible new yield record

2027 — the year that matters#

Event
🟨 SPARC first plasma and attempted scientific breakeven (Q > 1)
🟨 HH170 construction completes (Energy Singularity, China) — Q ≥ 10 target device
🟨 Astral Systems fusion-fission hybrid validation testing
🟩 ITER assembly continues; further sector modules

SPARC is the pivot point of the entire decade. Its design goal is Q > 2, with a nominal projection of Q ≈ 11 and ~140 MW of fusion power at standard confinement assumptions. DOE's roadmap names "Milestone 1: Q > 1, first demonstration in a U.S. fusion device" — which is SPARC by any reading.

What would count as success: any Q > 1. Even Q = 1.5 validates the high-field HTS thesis and confirms that confinement scaling extrapolates as predicted into a regime no one has operated in.

What would count as failure: Q substantially below 1 with no clear physics explanation; or a magnet, Divertor or Disruption event that costs years of repair.

The realistic middle case, and the one to expect: first plasma in 2027, a commissioning campaign through 2027–28, and the D-T breakeven attempt landing in 2028. Machines rarely reach design performance in their first campaign.

2028#

Event
🟥 Helion Orion delivers ≥50 MW to Microsoft — the most aggressive schedule in the industry
🟨 General Fusion LM26 program milestones conclude (funded through end-2028)
🟨 SPARC D-T campaign and sustained Q > 1 if not achieved in 2027
🟨 Avalanche Energy targets scientific and engineering net gain (company projection)
🟩 ITER: continued assembly toward 2034

On Helion. Its 2028 date is genuinely aspirational: it requires Orion to be built, commissioned, and to achieve net electricity in an approach that has never demonstrated Q > 1, on a machine whose predecessor is still a research device. That said, Helion is building the plant, running Polaris daily with D-T, and has a counterparty that ran diligence. Treat 2028 as the earliest conceivable date rather than the expected one.

What the industry itself expects in this window#

From the FIA survey:

  • First pilot plant: 28 of 47 companies say 2030–2035
  • First commercial electricity: 16 of 45 say 2031–2035
  • Scientific net gain: 24 of 31 say 2030–2035
  • Engineering net gain: 16 of 33 say 2030–2035

This is the window in which the sector's collective bet resolves. It is also where the materials and tritium gaps bite hardest: pilot plants designed in the late 2020s will be built before IFMIF-DONES and FPNS have produced power-plant-relevant datasets. Every pilot plant in this phase will be built on extrapolated materials data and will itself function as the materials experiment.

The most likely outcome#

  • One or two machines achieve Q > 1 in magnetic confinement (SPARC almost certainly; possibly HH170 or another)
  • At least one demonstration of fusion-derived electricity in some quantity — most plausibly from a pulsed, direct-conversion device rather than a thermal-cycle Tokamak
  • No plant achieves tritium self-sufficiency in this window
  • No plant runs at power for a sustained duty cycle
  • Several companies fail or pivot, as First Light Fusion already has

DOE's long-term milestones (5–10 years, i.e. 2031–2036)#

The roadmap's own list is a good proxy for what "demonstration phase" means technically:

  • Demonstrate component-relevant performance under fusion-prototypic neutron, heat and tritium loads simultaneously
  • Codify fusion-specific requirements into ASME-based standards
  • Validate tungsten-based and liquid-metal PFCs under prototypic conditions
  • Deliver predictive, uncertainty-quantified transport models
  • Demonstrate an integrated, tritium-self-sufficient fuel cycle sustaining continuous early-stage power-plant duty
  • Demonstrate a TRL 7 integrated blanket with TBR > 1.1, reliable heat removal, embedded diagnostics and maintainability
  • Validate closed Brayton/Rankine power cycles
  • Demonstrate automated controls and remote operations in steady-state and high-repetition-rate facilities
  • Realise plant-ready IFE target systems

Read that list carefully. Every item is an engineering demonstration, not a physics result. That is the correct shape for this decade.

Phase 4: 2040+ — Deployment, if it happens#

Target
🟩 EUROfusion: fusion electricity on the European grid by 2050
🟨 STEP delivering net electricity
🟥 Fleet deployment; learning-curve cost reduction
🟥 Tritium fleet bootstrapping via breeding surplus

This is where the doubling-time problem in Document 09 §3 becomes decisive. A fusion industry — as opposed to a fusion plant — requires each plant to breed enough surplus tritium to start the next one within a commercially tolerable period. The difference between TBR = 1.05 and TBR = 1.15 is the difference between doubling every few years and effectively not growing at all.

Nobody knows which side of that line real blankets will land on. It will not be known until UNITY-3, LIBRTI and ITER's TBM program produce data — i.e. the late 2020s at the earliest for components, and the 2040s for an integrated plant-scale answer.

Six scenarios#

A. Fast success (probability: low)#

SPARC hits Q > 5 in 2027. Helion's Orion delivers power in 2029. Blanket tests at UNITY-3 show TBR > 1.1 by 2029. ARC comes online in 2031 at ~$6,000/kW and performs. Capital floods in; the learning curve engages. Result: fusion is a real, growing energy source by the late 2030s.

B. Grinding success (probability: moderate — the most likely good outcome)#

SPARC achieves Q > 1 in 2027–28 but below projection. Helion slips to the early 2030s. ARC comes online mid-decade, over budget, at 50–60% availability, and teaches the industry what it got wrong. Materials data arrives from IFMIF-DONES and FPNS in the mid-2030s and forces design changes. Second-generation plants in the early 2040s are the first economic ones. Result: fusion contributes materially to the grid from the 2040s. Roughly the trajectory of nuclear fission, compressed.

C. Physics success, engineering stall (probability: moderate — the most likely bad outcome)#

SPARC works. ITER works. Everyone can make a burning plasma. But helium embrittlement at 10–15 dpa disqualifies the leading structural steels, blankets achieve TBR ~1.0 rather than 1.1, and availability with remote maintenance lands at 40%. Plants are built and are uneconomic. Result: fusion becomes a niche technology for specific high-value applications — space, submarines, isolated industrial heat — and never competes on the grid.

D. Consolidation shock (probability: moderate)#

A funding downturn coincides with a high-profile technical failure. Most of the 56 companies fail within three years, taking supply-chain capacity and the specialist workforce with them. Two or three survivors continue on government money. Result: a decade of delay, not a permanent failure. This has happened to fusion before.

E. The Chinese path (probability: genuinely uncertain)#

China's industrial throughput — CFEC's $2.1 bn capitalisation, ~$1.5 bn/yr public spending, 582-tonne magnets in test, complete domestic supply chains for REBCO, tungsten, lithium and cryogenics — produces a working demonstration plant before the West. Fusion technology leadership follows the manufacturing base, as it did for solar and batteries. Result: fusion arrives, and the industrial and geopolitical benefits accrue largely to China.

F. The surprise (probability: non-trivial, unforecastable)#

An approach currently classed as high-variance works far better than expected — Zap's coil-free Z-pinch, Realta's mirror with direct conversion, a fast-ignition gain breakthrough, or an aneutronic route nobody credits — and the mainstream roadmap becomes irrelevant.

Fusion's history strongly supports keeping weight on this scenario. The Stellarator was written off in 1969 and leads the world on triple product at long pulse in 2026. The magnetic mirror was cancelled in 1986 and set the field record in 2024. Nothing in fusion is permanently dead; concepts wait for an enabling technology.

The single most useful heuristic#

When you encounter a fusion timeline, ask one question:

What is the machine, and where is it right now?

  • SPARC is 80% assembled in a building in Devens, Massachusetts, with 18 magnets manufactured. A 2027 date is a schedule.
  • ITER's magnets are complete and its sector modules are being lowered into a pit in Provence. A 2034 date is a schedule.
  • A pilot plant with no intermediate machine and a 2032 date is a hypothesis.

Everything in fusion that has ever happened on time happened because the hardware already existed.
