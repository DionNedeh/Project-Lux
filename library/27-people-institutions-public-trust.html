1. The workforce problem is more acute than the materials problem#

Fusion's people constraint gets a fraction of the attention given to tritium or neutron damage, and on current trajectory it binds sooner.

The scale#

Year Direct employees (fusion companies)
2021 1,096
2022 1,545
2023 3,073
2024 4,107
2025 4,607
2026 5,606

Plus 10,497 supply-chain jobs supported (FIA 2026; both figures are undercounts, as not all companies report).

For context, the global fusion sector employed roughly 2,400 people in 2023, of whom nearly 40% held PhDs. That doctoral density is extraordinary and it is the problem: this is a workforce that takes eight to ten years to produce, and it cannot be scaled by hiring.

The pipeline is contracting exactly when demand is rising#

The published analysis (Accelerating the fusion workforce in the USA, Plasma Phys. Control. Fusion, 2025; arXiv:2501.03372) is blunt:

The workforce needs of the nascent fusion industry are growing at a rate that academic workforce development programs are not currently able to match.

The specific findings:

  • A contraction in PhD admissions in the 2024–2025 cycle, with universities unable to sustain previous intake levels
  • The projected consequence is a shortage of principal investigators and lead engineers in the early 2030s — precisely when commercial fusion plants are expected to come online
  • The US has historically relied on international talent, with international students comprising up to 72% of graduate student populations in relevant fields. That supply line is not currently being replenished by new enrolments
  • Funding pressure on the federal research agencies that support graduate students compounds it

Meanwhile, China is reported to be investing $6.5 billion and training 10,000 fusion engineers.

Who is doing something about it#

  • PPPL launched a four-year registered apprenticeship programme for technicians, open to anyone with a high school diploma or equivalent. This is arguably the most important item in this section: fusion does not only need PhDs. It needs vacuum technicians, cryogenic operators, precision welders, remote-handling operators and radiological control staff — roles that require training, not doctorates, and that the sector has almost entirely neglected.
  • The inaugural Fusion Innovation Bootcamp hosted 15 students from 15–26 June 2026 at Tennessee State University, themed "Fusion Supply Chain and Workforce Development" — notably at an HBCU, and notably focused on supply chain rather than plasma physics.
  • Oxford's EPSRC Centre for Doctoral Training in Fusion Power and equivalent European CDTs.
  • IAEA InFUSEd training programmes.
  • DOE's roadmap makes "Foster Talent by Enabling Fusion Workforce Pathways" an explicit Grow-pillar key action.

The industry's own assessment#

14 companies name skilled workforce availability a near-term challenge; 15 name it long-term. It is one of only three issues whose long-term score exceeds its near-term score — the sector expects this to get worse.

Demographics#

From the FIA survey: 37% scientists, 21% engineers, remainder other roles. 79% male, 21% female.

That gender split is worse than physics as a whole and considerably worse than engineering. For a sector that needs to roughly quadruple its headcount within a decade while competing with AI and semiconductors for the same graduates, drawing from a 21% share of one demographic is not only an equity question — it is a supply question.

The journals#

Fusion publishes in a small, well-defined set. Knowing which is which is useful for assessing a claim:

  • Nuclear Fusion (IAEA / IOP) — the field's flagship. Major experimental results and the ITER physics basis
  • Physics of Plasmas (AIP) — theory and the Wurzel-Hsu Lawson papers
  • Plasma Physics and Controlled Fusion (IOP) — theory, workforce studies
  • Journal of Plasma Physics (Cambridge) — where CFS published the SPARC physics basis and Type One published Infinity Two's blanket and fuel-cycle feasibility. Increasingly the venue where serious private companies publish
  • Fusion Engineering and Design (Elsevier) — the engineering journal: magnets, blankets, remote handling, power supplies
  • Fusion Science and Technology (ANS) — technology and tritium
  • Journal of Fusion Energy (Springer) — including codes and standards, and Disruption prediction benchmarks
  • Nuclear Materials and Energy — irradiation and plasma-facing components
  • Nature / Nature Energy / Nature Physics / Science — for results that cross into general interest: DeepMind's control papers, MAST-U's Super-X result, NIF Ignition prediction

The conferences#

  • IAEA Fusion Energy Conference (FEC) — biennial, the field's global gathering. FEC 2025 was the 30th, held in Chengdu, China, with participation from over 50 countries, international organisations, academia and private companies
  • APS Division of Plasma Physics — annual, the largest US meeting
  • SOFE (Symposium on Fusion Engineering), SOFT (Symposium on Fusion Technology), ISFNT (Fusion Nuclear Technology), TOFE (Technology of Fusion Energy)
  • EPS Conference on Plasma Physics

The IAEA's convening role#

The IAEA has no licensing authority over fusion but has taken the central coordinating position:

  • World Fusion Outlook — launched annually by Director General Rafael Mariano Grossi; the 2025 edition included a special focus on high-temperature superconducting magnets. The IAEA describes it as the definitive global reference on fusion R&D, plant concepts and commercialisation pathways for both sectors
  • World Fusion Energy Group — its second Ministerial Meeting took place alongside FEC 2025 in Chengdu
  • Technical Meeting on Experience in Codes and Standards for Fusion Technology (November 2025) — the main forum for the standards problem in Document 23
  • FENDL — the nuclear data library the whole field's neutronics rests on (Document 19)

The IAEA's 2025 framing — that fusion is "entering a new phase of implementation as it becomes a cornerstone of national energy strategies and industrial planning" — matches what Document 08 documents happening in every major national programme simultaneously.

3. Notable moves and the people question#

Fusion is a small field, and personnel moves carry real signal.

The most significant of 2026: on 12 August, Professor Dennis Whyte was appointed Chief Executive of the UK Atomic Energy Authority.

Whyte directed MIT's Plasma Science and Fusion Center, was central to the SPARC/ARC design lineage and the high-field HTS thesis, and co-founded the intellectual programme that produced Commonwealth Fusion Systems. Moving to run the UK's fusion agency — which is delivering STEP, a spherical Tokamak prototype power plant — places the leading advocate of compact high-field tokamaks at the head of a national programme built on a different concept.

Whichever way that tension resolves, it is a substantial transatlantic transfer of expertise, and it says something about where the UK sees its programme going.

Other names that recur across this dossier: Bob Mumgaard (CFS CEO), Michl Binderbauer (TAE), David Kirtley (Helion), Andrew Holland (FIA CEO, and author of the industry report's candid forewords), Pietro Barabaschi (ITER Director-General, who delivered the 2024 rebaseline), Darío Gil (US Under Secretary for Science, whose office produced the June 2026 roadmap), and Rafael Mariano Grossi (IAEA Director General).

Why this matters analytically: in a field of ~5,600 direct employees with a handful of people who have actually built a large fusion device, institutional knowledge is concentrated in individuals to an unusual degree. JET's operating team, TFTR's D-T experience, and W7-X's construction lessons live substantially in people, not documents. A generation of that expertise is retiring, and the workforce pipeline described in §1 is not replacing it at the required rate.

4. Public trust#

What the polling actually shows#

Europe — a survey across 21 European countries (data collected November 2023) found, after respondents were shown informational material:

  • 57% positive perceptions
  • 58% acceptance
  • 53% support expansion

United States — research finds fusion viewed positively across age, gender and partisan groups, which is unusual for any energy technology in the current US political environment. But the same research finds:

  • Most respondents concede they know little about the technology
  • Frequent misconceptions are common — including confusion with fission, with cold fusion, and about waste and accident risk
  • Support correlates strongly with trust in scientists, regulatory bodies and industry. Respondents sceptical of institutions do not trust them to give an honest assessment of fusion's advantages and risks

How to read this#

The good news is real: fusion polls better than fission essentially everywhere, and it polls well across the political spectrum, which almost nothing in energy does.

The fragility is also real. Support that rests on low knowledge plus institutional trust is support that can move quickly. It has two specific failure modes:

  1. Conflation with fission. A fission incident anywhere could contaminate fusion's public standing, because most people do not distinguish them. The industry's regulatory success in being treated as not a reactor (Document 11) helps legally and does nothing for public perception.
  2. Disappointment fatigue. Fusion has a fifty-year record of announced breakthroughs that produced no electricity. Each "breakthrough" headline that is later understood as a partial result spends credibility. NIF's ignition was widely reported as "net energy," which it was not on a wall-plug basis — and the correction, when the public eventually absorbs it, lands as a broken promise rather than as a clarification.

The evidence from actual siting#

Against those risks, the practical record so far is good, and it is documented in Document 24 §5: unanimous local approval in Chesterfield County, Virginia; a UK site transferred three and a half years early with a completed public consultation; a US public utility hosting a stellarator at a retired coal plant.

The pattern of siting at retired coal and industrial sites is doing a great deal of work here. Those communities have existing energy-industry employment, existing infrastructure, and an economic motive to welcome a replacement. It converts a "do you want a nuclear thing near you" question into a "do you want the jobs back" question.

What would help#

The clearest implication of the polling is that the sector's main public-communication risk is over-claiming, not under-communicating. Support is broad and shallow; it rests on trust in institutions; and it is spent by every gap between announcement and delivery.

Two structural changes are already imposing discipline: public-market listings — General Fusion now reports quarterly as GFUZ — and milestone-gated funding, which ties money to demonstrated results. Both make over-promising costly in a way that press releases never were.

The FIA's own conclusion applies here too: "With that increased investment comes increased scrutiny. Fusion companies are setting bold goals... For companies that cannot meet their objectives, market consequences will follow."

5. Round 4 evidence update: support is conditional#

The 2026 scoping review found only 14 eligible studies from 2005–2025 and describes positive attitudes as tentative and weakly held. The six-country conjoint experiment found 73.5% unconditional support for fusion against 71.5% for next-generation fission, while distance, plant size, jobs, fuel sourcing and electricity price altered project choices.

This qualifies the earlier “broad and shallow” conclusion with project-level evidence. Fusion’s label is an opening advantage, not permission for a site; social licence must be maintained through governance, delivery and correction. See Document 42.

6. Assessment#

The workforce is the constraint nobody priced. A sector needing to grow four- to five-fold within a decade, drawing on a talent pool that takes eight to ten years to produce, facing contracting PhD admissions and a disrupted international student pipeline, while competing with AI and semiconductors for the same people. Materials and tritium have identified facilities being built to address them. The workforce has apprenticeships and a bootcamp.

Institutional knowledge is concentrated and ageing. The people who built and ran JET, TFTR and W7-X are retiring, and their knowledge is substantially undocumented. JET's decommissioning programme running to 2040 is, among other things, an opportunity to capture some of it.

Public support is genuine, broad and shallow. It rests on institutional trust and low knowledge, which makes it stable in normal conditions and vulnerable to a single high-profile disappointment. The siting record so far is considerably better than the field's communication record.

The IAEA's convening function is worth more than it appears. In a field fragmenting into national industrial strategies, with Chinese companies declining to report to the industry survey and REBCO tape becoming an export-control question, the IAEA remains the venue where nuclear data, safety standards and codes can still be harmonised. That harmonisation is one of the cheapest available interventions and one of the few that gets harder the longer it is left.
