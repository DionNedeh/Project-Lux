Fusion's most economically decisive and least-analysed parameter.

LCOE is inversely proportional to capacity factor: a plant at 50% availability costs twice as much per MWh as the same plant at 100%. Fission achieves 90%+.

Where fusion availability goes#

Cause Frequency Duration
Divertor replacement Every 1–2 full-power years Months
Blanket module replacement Every 2–5 years Months
Planned remote inspection Annual Weeks
Disruption recovery Unplanned Days to months
Quench Rare Weeks to years

Fission achieves this with direct human access during outage and forty years of practice; fusion has neither (Remote Handling and Maintenance). For calibration: JET — Joint European Torus's first-wall replacement took 18 months of two-shift working, entirely remotely.

The state of the discipline#

DOE's roadmap asks, as a near-term deliverable, to "establish an initial RAMI handbook with metrics, availability targets, and design-for-maintainability guidance."

The industry has no agreed availability metrics, let alone data. Design studies routinely assume 75–85% for machines whose remote maintenance has never been demonstrated — one assumption that can move LCOE twofold.

Helios assumes 88% on one 84-day outage every two years (Overview of the Helios Design: A Practical Planar Coil Stellarator Fusion Power Plant), via sector-based maintenance: whole toroidal sectors withdrawn from between encircling coils that stay in place. A coherent answer to the rows above, and an assumption, not a demonstrated cycle time — no device has performed a sector exchange.

EU-DEMO's pre-concept work reports balance-of-plant availability could meet programme requirements (Tokamak Cooling Systems and Power Conversion System Options) — called preliminary by its own authors. That is the state of the evidence: targets and models, not operating records.

This is the likeliest reason a technically successful plant turns out commercially useless.

It also gates tritium self-sufficiency#

Availability factor Tritium self-sufficiency
< 10% Impossible for any η_f · f_b
> 30%, 1% ≤ η_f · f_b ≤ 2% Possible
> 50%, η_f · f_b > 2% Reasonable confidence

A plant spends downtime not breeding while its inventory decays, so low availability raises required TBR and start-up inventory together. FNSF, VNS and CTF are predicted below 30%, so the devices meant to demonstrate breeding blankets may not close their own fuel cycle. RAMI is not only an economics problem; it is a fuel-supply problem.

Meschini et al. 2023 put a sharper number on it: self-sufficiency in ARC- and STEP-class plants requires availability above 70%, alongside processing under 4 h and DIR. Their model's best achievable availability, on optimistic processing times, is 94%.
