The Investment Mechanics describes how private money enters fusion: venture rounds, milestone tranching, SPACs, the valley of death. It is a complete account of one half of the capital stack. This document covers the other half — public money acting as an investor rather than as a research funder — which the corpus had not treated at all.

The distinction matters because the two behave differently. A grant buys research. An investment buys a position, and expects the asset to exist afterwards.

1. The asymmetry#

The headline comparison, from CSIS:

Public fusion spending
US DOE Fusion Energy Sciences, 2020–2023 $2.34 bn
FES, 2025 $790 m
FES, 2026 $806 m (+2%)
China, public funds mobilised 2023–2025 ≥$6.5 bn, some estimates to $13 bn

China's public mobilisation over 2023–2025 runs at roughly three times the DOE Fusion Energy Sciences budget over the same period. The upper estimate is around four times larger again than the lower one, and the source does not resolve the range — so it is preserved here as a range, per CLAUDE.md §7.4.

Set against this, private capital tilts the other way: of roughly $15 bn of private fusion investment as of 2025, 53% went to US-based companies and 34% to China.

So the two systems are close to mirror images. The United States has a private-capital advantage and a public-capital deficit; China has the reverse. Whether that matters depends on which constraint actually binds — and this corpus's repeated finding, in Engineering Challenges and Roadmap to 2040, is that the binding constraints are materials qualification, tritium handling and neutron sources. Those are precisely the assets that private capital funds badly, because they are shared infrastructure with no single owner.

2. What public money buys that private money will not#

The CSIS analysis names National Ignition Facility ($3.5 bn of federal investment), PPPL and DIII-D as federally built infrastructure that private companies now depend on. That is the pattern: the state built the facilities, and private firms use them.

The structural reason is that a venture-backed company cannot justify funding an asset its competitors will also use. A neutron irradiation facility, a materials qualification database or a tritium handling licence has the economics of a public good — high fixed cost, non-rival use, no capturable return. This is why there is still no operating fusion-spectrum neutron source anywhere in the world, despite it being one of the field's most-cited gaps: it is exactly the asset the private market will not build and the public sector has not.

The CSIS figure that best exposes the mismatch is that only about 1.2% of FES funding across 2020–2023 was directed toward commercialisation. The public programme was, and largely remains, a science programme. Its output is knowledge and trained people — see People, Institutions and Public Trust — not deployable plant.

Read against this corpus's central conclusion, the asymmetry is pointed. If plasma physics is largely delivered and the remaining risk sits in metallurgy, tritium chemistry and project finance, then a public programme spending on plasma science is funding the solved problem.

3. Sovereign and pension capital enters directly#

The newer development is public and quasi-public capital entering private rounds rather than funding laboratories.

Two 2026 instances are reported in trade press:

  • Commonwealth Fusion Systems raised $1 bn from pension funds and sovereign wealth funds (July 2026), reported as a first for the sector.
  • The New Mexico State Investment Council, a state sovereign fund built on oil and gas revenue, backed a $1 bn Pacific Fusion project near Albuquerque (March 2026).

Warning

Single-outlet trade reporting Both are single-outlet trade reports, not filings or audited figures, and both are announced rather than verified deployed. They are recorded here because the structural point does not depend on the precise number, but the figures themselves should be treated as provisional until the next FIA report.

The structural point is about duration. Pension and sovereign funds hold liabilities measured in decades and are mandated to invest accordingly. Venture funds hold ten-year fund lives, which The Investment Mechanics identifies as badly matched to fusion's timeline. Capital whose horizon matches the asset is a genuinely different input, not merely more of the same — and if it persists, it changes which companies can afford to be slow.

The New Mexico case adds a second pattern: a resource-revenue fund diversifying out of hydrocarbons into the technology that would displace them. That is a recognisable sovereign-fund strategy, and it implies a political logic — regional industrial development — quite separate from the energy case in Fusion and Climate.

3a. The public-good argument, stated properly#

The strongest case for public capital in fusion is not industrial policy or competition with China. It is a straightforward market-failure argument, and it is worth setting out precisely because it identifies which investments the state should make rather than arguing for more money generally.

An asset qualifies when three things hold: every serious developer needs it, none can exclude the others from benefiting, and no one can capture enough of the return to justify the cost alone. Under those conditions private capital will rationally under-provide no matter how much of it there is — so the $15 bn of private money in the sector cannot fix the problem by growing.

Three assets in this corpus meet the test:

  • A fusion-spectrum neutron source. There is still none operating anywhere in the world. Every materials qualification programme needs one; none of Commonwealth Fusion Systems, TAE Technologies or Helion Energy can justify building one alone, and whoever did would qualify their competitors' steels alongside their own.
  • A materials qualification database. Irradiation data is slow, expensive and cumulative. Its value rises with sharing, which is exactly the property that destroys the incentive to fund it privately.
  • Tritium handling capacity and licensed inventory. Constrained, strategically held, and discussed under Tritium Breeding Ratio (TBR).

These are precisely the constraints this corpus repeatedly identifies as binding. So the mismatch is sharper than a budget comparison suggests: it is not simply that US public funding is smaller than China's, but that only about 1.2% of it went toward commercialisation across 2020–2023, while the assets that actually gate deployment are the ones only public money will build.

The counter-argument deserves stating. Public infrastructure programmes are slow, and a neutron source commissioned in the 2030s may arrive after the companies that needed it have either succeeded without it or failed. Roadmap to 2040 treats this timing problem directly. Public capital is not merely delayed private capital; it moves on political cycles, and those cycles are shorter than fusion's.

3b. What the industry itself declares as public money#

There is a second, sharper way to see the imbalance, and it comes from the sector's own accounting rather than from an external comparison.

The FIA 2026 report breaks its $14.2 bn cumulative total into $13,261,437,000 private and $980,706,000 public. Public money is therefore about 7% of all funding the fusion industry declares having received.

That number is worth holding next to the DOE figure. Fusion Energy Sciences alone spent $806 m in 2026 — a single year of one national programme, approaching the entire public contribution the global industry reports across its whole history. The two are not measuring the same thing, and that is precisely the point: the FIA counts public money that reached companies, whereas the FES budget is largely spent inside national laboratories and universities.

So the 7% figure does not mean states are absent from fusion. It means state money and private money are largely funding different things — laboratories and science on one side, companies and plant on the other — with comparatively little flow between them. The 1.2% commercialisation share of FES funding is the same fact seen from the public side.

This has a consequence for how The Private Fusion Industry should be read. The private industry is not a spin-out of the public programme in any financial sense; it is a parallel system that inherits the public programme's knowledge and people while raising its capital elsewhere. When People, Institutions and Public Trust describes the workforce pipeline as a constraint, that transfer is most of what the public programme currently contributes to commercial fusion.

4. What this does not settle#

Whether the Chinese figure is comparable. $6.5–13 bn of "mobilised public funds" may include state enterprise capital, provincial commitments and infrastructure that Western accounting would not place in a fusion budget. The comparison with a US line-item appropriation is not like-for-like, and no source in this corpus reconciles them. ENN Science and Technology Development's funding, already a known conflict, illustrates how hard Chinese fusion figures are to pin down.

Whether public investment converts. The CSIS recommendation of a one-time $10 bn increase is attributed to unnamed industry experts and is advocacy, not a costed programme. Nothing here demonstrates that additional public money produces deployable plant rather than more research.

Where the liability sits. If a state fund backs a plant that fails, the loss is public. The waste liability described in Fusion Waste: Classification, Clearance and Disposal is likewise borne by the operator and, ultimately, the state. Public capital in fusion carries a downside that the private-market framing of The Investment Mechanics does not capture, and no source in this corpus quantifies it.

Note

Confidence Medium. The budget lines are firm; the Chinese comparison carries an unresolved 2× range and a definitional problem; the sovereign and pension investments rest on single-outlet trade reporting. The structural argument is more robust than any individual figure in it.
