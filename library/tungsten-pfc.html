The consensus plasma-facing material: highest melting point of any metal (3,422 °C), lowest sputtering yield of any practical candidate, low tritium retention.

Its own limits#

  • Recrystallises above ~1300 °C, losing strength permanently
  • DBTT around 400 °C, pushed higher by irradiation — brittle when cold, weak when hot, with a narrow usable band
  • High-Z radiator — core tungsten concentration must stay below roughly 10⁻⁵ or the plasma quenches
  • Transmutes to rhenium and osmium under neutron flux, changing properties over life
  • Helium implantation produces a nanostructured surface "fuzz" that degrades performance

The supply exposure#

Over 80% of global tungsten supply is controlled by non-Western entities, China dominant in both mining and processing. Fusion enters this market needing not commodity tungsten but precision-manufactured, actively cooled monoblocks in quantities nobody has produced. See Component Manufacturing.

The alternative#

Liquid metal PFCs — flowing lithium or tin. A liquid cannot be neutron-damaged, cannot recrystallise, and self-heals after erosion. If they work, both the tungsten supply problem and the tungsten materials problem disappear at once.

Designing around the DBTT#

Helios shows how the narrow usable band above gets engineered around (Overview of the Helios Design: A Practical Planar Coil Stellarator Fusion Power Plant). Its Divertor targets are 51,000 tessellated hexagonal tungsten tiles, 2.5 cm across, cooled by closed-loop helium impingement jets and mounted on a vanadium-alloy support structure, under an assumed 10 MW/m². The coolant reaches them already pre-heated by the blanket to above tungsten's ductile-to-brittle transition temperature — the cold end of the band is removed by routing the coolant, not by choosing a different metal.

It also illustrates the manufacturing exposure concretely: 51,000 precision tiles per plant is the kind of quantity the supply concern above is about. This is a preconceptual design; no such target has been built.
