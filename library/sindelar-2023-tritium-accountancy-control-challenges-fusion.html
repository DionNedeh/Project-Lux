The current assessment, presented to the INMM & ESARDA joint annual meeting — the safeguards profession's own forum — and recapping the tritium panel of the Fusion Energy and Nonproliferation Workshop held at PPPL. Funded jointly by DOE NNSA's Office of Defense Nuclear Nonproliferation and the DOE Office of Fusion Energy Science, which is itself informative about who is asking the question.

The regulatory position, precisely#

This is the paper that lets the vault state tritium's status exactly rather than by implication:

  • Until 2011 tritium was regarded as a special nuclear material (SNM) by the US DOE.
  • At present it is categorised as "other nuclear material" for Nuclear Material Control and Accountability purposes, per DOE-STD-1194-2019, driven by DOE Order 474.2A (7 February 2023).
  • Neither the US NRC nor the IAEA consider tritium an SNM.
  • Prospective fusion systems — fission/fusion hybrids excepted — would not require special fissionable materials, and so "would not be subject to safeguards per the IAEA".
  • Consequently neither the IAEA nor the NRC prescribe material control and accountability requirements for tritium at all.

Warning

The breeding margin is the diversion quantity The sharpest finding, and one that reframes a number this vault has only treated as an engineering requirement. Sustainability requires breeding 10–20% above what is burned — and the paper states that "this additional 10–20% of bred tritium could become a source for diversion of tritium for use other than the specific fusion device at which it is bred."

The tritium panel puts it more starkly: the excess, bred minus burned minus losses, "is where any diverted tritium would have to come from, otherwise, the fusion process will not be sustainable."

So TBR > 1.1 is simultaneously the condition for fusion existing as an energy source and the quantity a safeguards regime would have to watch. The margin cannot be designed away without destroying the fuel cycle.

Warning

The two measurements fusion most needs do not exist The table's own note: "Measurement methods including indirect methods using neutron flux for tritium consumption by the fusion reaction and for tritium production in the blanket would need to be developed."

That is the whole of the fusion-specific problem in one sentence. Burn-up and breeding — the two terms the fusion-modified balance adds (Tritium Accountancy in Fusion Systems Eq. 5) — have no measurement method. Everything in the table above measures tritium in a pipe or a bed, not tritium being made in a blanket or consumed in a plasma.

The tritium panel's findings#

  • ITER's total in-process tritium inventory is expected to be approximately 1 kg. Note this is in-process inventory, a different quantity from the start-up inventory of 11 kg that Abdou computes for a 3 GW plant.
  • Commercial D-T plants are put at several hundred grams up to 1 kg in-process each, with "hundreds to thousands" of plants globally at scale.
  • Steady state is not reached until permeation and hold-up losses have occurred, and is plant-specific — so early-life accountancy is measuring a system that is still filling its own walls.
  • Accountancy error spreads through successive input, burn, output and breed steps.
  • The international staff scale for monitoring "would be substantial".
  • Static-system inventory methods named: installed metal hydride beds, installed molecular sieve beds, sealed confinement vessels; loss detection by inventory-difference control limits and statistical sampling plans.

Access limitations#

Inspected in full. The nine-page paper was captured to raw/ from the INMM resource server and extracted; everything above was read from that extract. Publicly posted by INMM; the paper carries a US Government public-access licence under the DOE Public Access Plan.

Note

The accuracy figures are twenty years old Table 1 is reproduced from a 2006 paper (Nishi et al.) inside a 2023 assessment. The vault records them as the best compiled figures it holds, not as current instrument performance — two decades of spectroscopy and mass spectrometry sit between that table and now. Nishi et al. 2006 is not held in raw/, so these accuracies reach the vault at second hand.

Not inspected, and all cited by this paper as the primary record behind its findings:

  • The Fusion Energy and Nonproliferation Workshop Summary Report, Revision 1, 7 April 2023 (PPPL-hosted) — the source of the tritium panel findings quoted above.
  • Nishi et al. (2006) — the origin of every accuracy figure.
  • Flynn & Larsen, Investigating the Application of Kalman Filters for Real-time Accountancy in Fusion Fuel Cycles, Fusion Engineering and Design 176 (2022) 113037 — directly addressing the continuous-flow problem, and the obvious next capture on this subject.
  • DOE-STD-1194-2019, DOE O 474.2A and DOE-STD-1129-2015, the governing US standards.

What this source does not establish#

  • No measurement of anything. This is an assessment of challenges and a recap of a workshop panel. It reports no experiment and no plant data.
  • It does not propose a regime. It "discusses the practicality of establishing a regulatory framework for safeguards controls" — it does not conclude that one should exist or specify one.
  • The accuracies are not fusion-demonstrated. They are generic tritium-handling instrument figures; none was obtained on an operating fusion fuel cycle, because none exists.
  • US-centric. The regulatory analysis is DOE and NRC; it establishes that the IAEA does not cover tritium, but says nothing about what other national regimes require.
