The time a plant takes to breed enough surplus tritium to double its stored inventory — and so to supply the start-up charge of the next plant. It is the quantity that converts a single working reactor into a fleet, which makes it an industry-scaling parameter rather than a plant one.

Why it is a design requirement and not an outcome#

With no external tritium supply worth relying on, plant n+1 is fuelled by plant n. A long doubling time therefore caps the deployment rate of an entire industry, independently of how well any single machine performs — the link to build-out timelines.

Meschini et al. 2023 show the requirement trades directly against burn efficiency:

Target t_d Verdict
1 year Self-sufficiency "highly unlikely" below 1% tritium burn efficiency
5 years "Much easier to achieve"

So a short doubling time is bought with burn efficiency, plant availability above 70%, processing times under 4 hours and direct internal recycling — not with breeding ratio alone.

Declared values#

Design t_d Source
ST-E1 152 days 086004
Generic reference 5 years Abdou et al.

ST-E1's 152 days is strikingly short against Abdou's five-year reference, and it is a designed figure, not a measured one. It is also computed under a 100% availability assumption — see Availability and RAMI — which is the most optimistic input available and sits above the >70% that Meschini et al. require.

Warning

Trapping stretches it by half again Meschini et al. 2025 find doubling time rises +50% once trapping, damage-induced traps and component replacement are modelled, for an ARC-class plant. No source applies that correction to ST-E1, and this vault does not apply it either.

No doubling time has ever been measured. Every figure here is the output of a system-level model of a plant that does not exist.
