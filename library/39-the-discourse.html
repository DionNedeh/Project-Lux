Project Lux · How fusion is talked about, why the talk keeps going wrong, and how to read a press release

1. Why this belongs in a technical dossier#

Fusion has a communication problem that is not incidental to its technical problem. It has shaped funding cycles, distorted public expectations, and — per the polling in Document 27 — left the field with support that is broad, positive, and built on almost no knowledge, which is exactly the kind of support that collapses when a promise visibly fails.

A survey of fusion that reports only what the field says about itself is incomplete. This document covers what its critics say, why the "thirty years away" joke is both true and misleading, and how to evaluate a fusion claim without a plasma physics degree.

2. The joke, and its actual history#

Fusion is thirty years away, and always will be.

The origin is real. At the first Geneva Atoms for Peace conference in 1955, Homi Bhabha predicted in his presidential address that a method of controlled fusion would be found "within the next two decades." Seventy-one years later it has not been commercialised.

The joke is therefore empirically earned, and any account of fusion that waves it away is not being straight.

But the causal story is not what the joke implies#

The joke implies that fusion is perpetually receding because the problem is harder than anyone admits. The evidence in this dossier points somewhere else.

Exhibit A — the funding record. The 1976 US ERDA plan modelled fusion development against several funding levels. With aggressive funding, a demonstration plant was projected for roughly 1990. Actual US appropriations tracked the plan's lowest curve — the one labelled, in the original, as the level at which fusion never arrives. Fusion followed the schedule it was funded for.

Exhibit B — the triple product record. Document 26 establishes that the highest triple product ever achieved in a Tokamak was set by TFTR in February 1995 and has not been exceeded in thirty-one years. That is not a physics plateau. Between TFTR's shutdown in 1997 and SPARC's assembly in the 2020s, no machine capable of exceeding it was built anywhere in the world. The field spent that period building ITER, and ITER was late.

Exhibit C — the failure record. Document 25 examines seven major fusion failures. Four were manufacturing, one was politics, one was diagnostics, one was a business decision. None was a failure of plasma physics.

The defensible version of the story is therefore: fusion was underfunded for forty years, and the delay is substantially a funding delay rather than a physics delay. That is a better argument than the joke — and it is also, uncomfortably, an argument that predicts the current era should go faster, which is a falsifiable claim now being tested.

3. The skeptical case, at its strongest#

Steelmanning the critics is more useful than dismissing them, and the strongest version has three parts.

3.1 The engineering is harder than the physics, and it is barely started#

The core critique, and it is correct:

The challenges of constructing and operating a fusion-based power plant could be more severe than the physics challenge of generating the fireballs in the first place — requiring materials that can withstand temperatures of millions of degrees for years on end while being constantly bombarded by high-energy nuclear particles that turn ordinary materials brittle and radioactive.

And the sharper version: no one has achieved sustained Q > 5 operation, and even success with SPARC would leave the engineering problems — tritium, neutrons, continuous operation — unsolved.

This dossier agrees, and says so throughout. Document 09 ranks materials and tritium above plasma physics; the industry's own survey ranks neutron-resilient materials and tritium self-sufficiency as its top two long-term challenges with plasma science seventh; the IEA's 2026 assessment states that "the fuel cycle and materials are still not ready for scale-up."

When the critics, the industry and the IEA all say the same thing, it is probably true.

3.2 The specific claims that do not survive scrutiny#

Critics are right about several things the sector says loosely:

  • D-³He requires helium-3, which is rarer than tritium. True. See Document 22 — terrestrial supply is tens of kilograms per year, and lunar regolith concentrations are parts per billion
  • D-D still produces significant neutrons. True. "Aneutronic-leaning" is not aneutronic
  • No device has demonstrated net gain in magnetic confinement. True as of August 2026
  • NIF's "net energy" was target gain, not wall-plug gain. True, and the wall-plug figure was around 0.02 (Document 26)

3.3 The opportunity-cost argument#

Money and political attention spent on a technology that integrated assessment models say will not be economically selected until around 2070 (Document 35) could deploy solar, wind, storage and transmission now, when the emissions matter.

The honest counterargument is about fungibility and magnitude, not about merit: global fusion investment is a small fraction of one year's clean energy capital expenditure, and hyperscaler balance sheets and deep-tech venture capital do not otherwise buy transmission lines. But the critique is not silly, and it deserves to be answered rather than dismissed.

4. The "this time is different" case, and how much of it holds#

Five arguments are made for why the current era is genuinely discontinuous. Assessed honestly:

Argument Verdict
Ignition has been achieved Holds. Eleven times, gain to 4.13. The seventy-year physics threshold is crossed. Not an argument about power plants
HTS magnets changed the economics Holds. B⁴ scaling is real, 20 T is demonstrated, and it is why every compact concept exists
Private capital exceeds public Holds, with a caveat. $13.26 bn private vs $0.98 bn public. But the companies' own estimate of further need implies over $100 bn
Machines are being built again Holds, and is the strongest argument. SPARC 80% assembled, ITER magnets complete, BEST under construction, STEP with a site. The thing that stopped for twenty-five years has restarted
Regulation is settled Holds. The NRC byproduct rule and the UK's statutory exclusion removed a real financing risk at zero cost

What does not hold: that any of this addresses materials, tritium breeding, availability or first-of-a-kind cost. Those are unchanged, unretired, and are what the field itself says are hardest.

The fair summary: the current era is genuinely different in physics, in capital, in construction and in regulation — and genuinely unchanged in the four things that determine whether a fusion plant is economic.

5. How to read a fusion claim#

A practical checklist, assembled from the failure modes documented throughout this dossier.

The seven questions#

1. Which Q? Scientific, target, wall-plug or engineering? NIF's record shot was target gain 4.13 and wall-plug gain roughly 0.02. JET's famous 0.67 is a transient peak; the harmonised value for the same shot is 0.27. If the type of Q is not stated, assume the most flattering one was chosen. (Document 01 §5)

2. Is there a confinement time? A temperature is not a triple product. A neutron count is not a triple product. ZETA reported neutrons in 1958 and retracted in May because they were beam-target, not thermonuclear. Any claim reporting temperature or yield without confinement time is incomplete in exactly the way that produced fusion's worst public failure. (Document 25)

3. Where is the hardware right now? The single most useful question. SPARC is 80% assembled in a building in Massachusetts with 18 magnets manufactured — a 2027 date is a schedule. A pilot plant with no intermediate machine and a 2032 date is a hypothesis. Everything in fusion that has ever happened on time happened because the hardware already existed. (Document 13)

4. What is the tritium plan? Any D-T company that has not stated how it reaches TBR > 1.1 has not confronted its hardest problem. There is no market to buy tritium from. (Document 09)

5. What is the materials plan? A power plant first wall sees ~15 dpa/year. There is no operating fusion-spectrum neutron source. Every pilot plant will be built on extrapolated data — a company that acknowledges this is being straight; one that does not has not thought about it.

6. Is it peer-reviewed? CFS published SPARC's physics basis as a seven-paper Journal of Plasma Physics special issue. Type One published Infinity Two including blanket and fuel-cycle feasibility. Proxima published Stellaris. Avalanche publishes in Nature and Physics of Plasmas. Publishing is costly and invites attack, which is precisely what makes it informative.

7. Has an independent party with money at risk validated it? DOE Milestone Program certification. A signed PPA from a counterparty that ran diligence. A milestone-tranched round where capital releases on demonstrated results. These are stronger signals than any press release.

Four phrases that should slow you down#

  • "Breakthrough" — used for results spanning ten orders of magnitude of significance
  • "Net energy" — see question 1
  • "Unlimited clean energy" — see Document 22 on Lithium-6, tungsten, beryllium and helium
  • "Too cheap to meter" — the phrase originally applied to fission in 1954. The best independent analysis puts mature fusion LCOE at $110–145/MWh and explicitly warns policymakers against exactly this framing (Document 10)

6. Why the discourse is improving#

Three structural changes are imposing discipline that exhortation never did:

Public markets. General Fusion trades as GFUZ. Quarterly reporting, audited statements, and a share price that responds to missed milestones within minutes. The FIA's framing: "the day-to-day financial progress of companies able to be tracked simply on a minute-to-minute ticker symbol."

Milestone tranching. Capital released against published, falsifiable technical targets — DOE's Milestone Program, Pacific Fusion's $1 bn Series A, General Fusion's LM26 milestones of 1 keV, 10 keV and Lawson. You cannot tranche against a vague claim. (Document 34)

A shared quantitative dataset. Wurzel and Hsu's harmonised Lawson analysis gives the field a common, definition-consistent basis for comparing results across machines and decades. It is why this dossier can state that JET's famous 0.67 and its harmonised 0.27 are both correct and measure different things.

And a fourth, from the FIA's own conclusion, which is as blunt an institutional statement as the field has produced:

With that increased investment comes increased scrutiny. Fusion companies are setting bold goals. As we enter the second half of the decade, companies will start to deliver on those goals. For companies that cannot meet their objectives, market consequences will follow.

7. Assessment#

The joke is earned and the diagnosis behind it is wrong. Fusion was late because it was underfunded and because no machines were built for twenty-five years, not because the physics kept receding. The triple-product record standing since 1995 is the cleanest evidence available.

The skeptics are right about the current substance. Materials, tritium, continuous operation and first-of-a-kind cost are unsolved, and SPARC succeeding would not solve them. The industry, the IEA and the DOE roadmap all say so.

The optimists are right that something changed. Ignition, HTS, private capital at scale, settled regulation, and — most importantly — machines under construction again after a quarter-century gap.

Both positions are compatible, and holding them simultaneously is the correct state. Fusion is much more likely to work than it was in 2015, and it is not close to being an energy source, and the gap between those two statements is where the entire 2030s will be spent.

The most useful discipline for anyone following this field is the one this dossier has tried to apply throughout: separate what has been demonstrated from what has been designed from what has been announced — and ask, every time, where the hardware is.

"No long-lived waste"#

A specific instance of the pattern this document describes. The claim is true in the narrow sense — fusion produces no transuranics and should avoid high-level waste — and misleading in the broad one, because it omits a substantially larger volume of low- and intermediate-level waste whose disposal class depends on impurity control and on jurisdiction. Fusion Waste: Classification, Clearance and Disposal gives the qualified version.
