The D-T reaction produces helium. It thermalises in the plasma and, unlike the neutron, **stays there**.

Helium is not a contaminant in the ordinary sense — inert, low-Z, radiating little. Its problem is **dilution**: every helium nucleus displaces fuel, and fusion power goes as the square of fuel density. A few percent helium is a direct multiplicative penalty on output.

The literature is unambiguous: control of helium ash is *"one of the key issues affecting the performance and achievable burn time of a fusion reactor."*

## The second-order consequence

**The helium fraction caps achievable tritium burn efficiency**, because core dilution limits fusion performance per unit of fuel circulated. Poorer ash removal means more tritium circulating for the same power → larger tritium inventory → higher cost and a tighter safety case. It connects directly to [[tritium-breeding-ratio]].

## Removal

**The only significant pathway out is pumping at the [[divertor]].** Helium must diffuse from core to edge, cross the separatrix, travel the scrape-off layer, and be captured before recycling back.

Helium is also difficult to pump — it does not condense at 4 K, requiring **charcoal adsorption panels** in compound cryopumps.

Approaches: selective helium pumping, divertor geometry optimised for helium compression, and pellet-based impurity capture. **It remains an open reactor-design question**, and it appears in the ARIES studies' list of critical physics areas alongside MHD stability and heat exhaust.
