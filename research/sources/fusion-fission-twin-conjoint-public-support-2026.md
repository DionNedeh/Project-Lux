Civera and colleagues fielded a twin conjoint experiment in **May 2025** across France, Germany, Italy, the United Kingdom, Spain and Poland. Respondents repeatedly chose between hypothetical next-generation nuclear projects whose attributes varied, allowing the study to separate the technology label from project characteristics.

Unconditional support was high for both technologies: **73.5% for fusion and 71.5% for next-generation fission**. The difference is real but small, so “fusion” is not an overriding public-consent advantage. Distance from homes, plant size, local employment, domestic or European fuel sourcing, and electricity-price effects all shaped preferences. Low electricity prices were especially important. Across the six proposed packages, **60% rejected none**, while **3.8% rejected all**.

The findings demonstrate stated preferences in a survey experiment, not permission for any real site. Hypothetical packages omit much of the institutional history, contested evidence and local mobilisation that appear once a project has an address. They are nevertheless stronger than a single approve/disapprove question because respondents must trade attributes against one another.

For [[42-public-acceptability-siting-social-licence|Document 42]], the decisive lesson is that social licence is partly designed through project choices. Proximity cannot be communicated away, but local jobs, credible fuel governance, scale and bill consequences can be governed. The paper’s fusion–fission comparison also cautions communicators against assuming that favourable fusion imagery survives concrete cost and siting details. DOI: **10.1016/j.enpol.2025.115007**.
