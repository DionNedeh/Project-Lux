# Survivorship: Failure, Retrenchment and the Shape of the Industry

[[21-the-graveyard]] catalogues failed *concepts* — cold fusion, Polywell, migma, the cancelled
machines. This document covers failed and retrenching *companies*.

> [!note] This deepens existing coverage rather than opening new ground
> The Round 5 gap analysis initially recorded company failure as **absent**, on a probe for
> `bankrupt`, `insolvent` and `ceased trading` that returned zero pages of 241. That probe was
> wrong, in the same way the Round 4 brief's AI/ML probe was wrong: the corpus discusses this
> subject in different vocabulary. [[00-executive-summary]] already states that the FIA survey is
> **"a survey of survivors and optimists"**; [[07-private-fusion-industry]] records that **three
> companies dropped out** of the 2026 survey and treats [[first-light-fusion]] as the instructive
> case; [[10-economics-and-markets]] states that most companies will not raise their share. The
> verdict should have been *partial*. What this document adds is **primary sourcing** for two 2025
> episodes and one case the corpus did not have at all.

The methodological point is established, and worth restating precisely because it governs how every
funding figure in this corpus should be read. The
[[fia-global-fusion-industry-report-2026|FIA Global Fusion Industry Report]] surveys companies
**that exist and respond**. Its $14.2 bn cumulative total, its count of 56 companies and its
year-on-year growth curve are measured over survivors. Its own CEO puts the expectation plainly:
*"few expect there to be 56 companies"* in a commercial fusion industry.

Two documented 2025 cases, newly sourced in this round, show the shape of what the survey does not
capture — and they are different shapes.

## 1. Abandoning the goal: First Light Fusion

In March 2025 [[first-light-fusion]]
[[first-light-fusion-strategic-update-2025|announced]] that it would **not continue** plans to
develop a fusion power plant. **Machine 4**, its proposed ignition demonstration plant — developed
with [[ukaea]] and Tractebel — was discontinued.

The company did not close. It pivoted to supplying its **amplifier** technology to the wider
inertial fusion industry and to non-fusion R&D in space and defence, describing the new model as
"capital-light, high-margin". It had raised roughly $140 m.

This is the important failure mode, and it is invisible in a funding table. First Light still exists,
still appears in industry surveys, still has fusion technology and customers. What it no longer has
is a **plan to generate power**. A count of fusion companies registers no change; the count of
organisations attempting fusion energy falls by one, and [[05-inertial-confinement-fusion]] loses a
named power-plant programme.

Note also what the company's own statement does not contain: any account of *why*. There is no
technical or financial reasoning given for dropping Machine 4. The retrenchment is documented; its
cause is not, and this corpus should not supply one.

## 2. Near-death and rescue: General Fusion

In May 2025 [[general-fusion]]
[[general-fusion-layoffs-2025|laid off at least 25% of its staff]] while short of cash. It was 23
years old and had raised **$440 m**; its most recent round had been **$22.66 m**, closed in July
2024. Backers included Jeff Bezos, Temasek and BDC Capital.

The detail that makes this case analytically valuable is the **timing**. The layoffs came days after
[[lm26]] successfully compressed plasma — a real technical milestone. The CEO's own explanation
pointed outward, at conditions rather than results: *"today's funding landscape is more challenging
than ever as investors and governments navigate a rapidly shifting and uncertain political and
market climate."*

The company subsequently recovered. It completed a SPAC merger with Spring Valley Acquisition Corp
III on 10 July 2026 and now trades on Nasdaq as "GFUZ" — the first publicly listed fusion company.
So this is a **near-failure and recovery**, not a death, and must be read as one.

But the structural finding survives the recovery: **technical progress did not produce capital.** A
milestone and a payroll crisis occurred in the same week. Whatever couples results to funding in
this sector, it is loose enough that a company can hit its technical target and still be unable to
pay staff.

## 3. What this does to the industry's numbers

Three corrections follow for how this corpus reads its own funding data.

**Growth curves measure survivors.** The FIA's cumulative curve rising to $14.2 bn is real, but it
is a curve through companies still present to be surveyed. It cannot fall when a company stops, and
it does not net out abandoned programmes. It is a measure of capital *raised*, not capital
*productively deployed*, and it should not be read as a proxy for progress.

**Dropouts are counted, but not characterised.** The 2026 survey records **6 new entrants and 3
dropouts** against 56 companies — so the FIA does track exit. What no source in this corpus supplies
is *why* those three left, or what became of them. Exit is a number in the dataset and a blank
everywhere else.

**Company counts overstate diversity of attempt.** First Light is the sharper case, because it did
**not** drop out — it remains in the survey while no longer pursuing a power plant. The meaningful
denominator for "how many organisations are trying to build a fusion power plant" is therefore
smaller than 56 by an unknown margin, and dropout counts do not reveal it.

**Retrenchment is more common than failure, and harder to see.** Neither 2025 case was a
bankruptcy. One was a pivot, one a near-miss followed by a public listing. Both would be invisible
in a dataset tracking only company existence and cumulative funding — which is exactly what the
sector's principal dataset tracks.

This is the same pattern [[39-the-discourse]] identifies elsewhere: the field's public numbers are
constructed in ways that make them go up.

## 3a. The industry expects most of these companies to fail

The most useful thing about the survivorship problem is that the field's own trade body states the
expectation openly. The [[fusion-industry-association]]'s CEO, in the 2026 report's conclusion:

> *"When we emerge into a fully commercial fusion industry, few expect there to be 56 companies in
> it. The race is on."*

This is not a sceptic's framing. It is the industry association's, published in the same document
that reports $14.2 bn of cumulative funding, and it should change how the funding figure is read.
The FIA is not claiming 56 viable companies; it is reporting 56 *attempts*, and saying plainly that
most will not survive.

[[10-economics-and-markets]] reaches the same conclusion from the capital side: the sum of the
companies' own stated funding requirements substantially exceeds any plausible supply of capital,
so most will not raise their share. Two independent routes — the trade body's expectation and the
arithmetic of required capital — arrive at the same place.

**What follows for this corpus is a reading rule.** Company-level projections should be treated as
conditional on survival, and survival is not the base case for any individual company. When
[[07-private-fusion-industry]] records that a company targets a plant in the 2030s, the honest
reading is *"targets, conditional on being one of the few that remain"*. That conditional is
absent from almost every company timeline in the corpus, and from essentially all press coverage —
which is the mechanism [[39-the-discourse]] describes.

It also reframes what the two 2025 cases are. First Light and General Fusion are not anomalies
against a background of success. They are early instances of the outcome the industry's own trade
body says is normal, and the notable thing about them is not that they happened but that they are
the only two this corpus can document in detail.

## 4. What happens to the technology

When a fusion company narrows or stops, its technology does not necessarily vanish — and this is
genuinely under-documented.

First Light's amplifiers survived the abandonment of Machine 4 and became the business. That is
technology surviving its parent programme. But the corpus has no systematic treatment of what
happens to intellectual property, staff or hardware when a fusion company fails — patents and IP
were probed during this round and returned two pages across 241. Given that
[[27-people-institutions-public-trust]] identifies the trained-workforce pipeline as a constraint,
where people go after a retrenchment is a real question this corpus cannot currently answer.

## 5. What this does not establish

**Two cases are not a base rate.** This document does not support any claim about how often fusion
companies fail, because no source here surveys failures systematically. Both cases are drawn from
2025 and from Western companies with press coverage; failures in less-covered markets would not
appear.

**Neither case tests the physics.** First Light's retrenchment was a business decision whose
reasoning is undisclosed; General Fusion's crisis was explicitly attributed to funding conditions.
Nothing here is evidence that any fusion approach does not work.

**The recovery may be the more important half.** General Fusion went from near-insolvency to a
public listing in fourteen months. That says something about capital availability in 2026 which may
matter more than the layoffs did — and which [[34-investment-mechanics]] and
[[44-state-capital-and-the-public-balance-sheet]] are better placed to judge.
