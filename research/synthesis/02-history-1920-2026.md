# 02 — History: 1920 to 2026

*Project Lux · A century of fusion, including the parts that went wrong*

---

## Introduction: the shape of the story

Fusion's history has an unusual structure. Most technologies proceed from discovery to demonstration to deployment in decades. Fusion has spent a century in the first two phases, and the reason is instructive: **fusion was understood before it was possible, and it was weaponised before it was controlled.**

The physics was essentially settled by 1939. The first release of fusion energy on Earth was a hydrogen bomb in 1952. Every year since has been an attempt to do that slowly, in a box, without destroying the box.

The story divides into six eras:

1. **1920–1945 — Astrophysics.** Fusion is discovered as the answer to a stellar puzzle.
2. **1946–1958 — Secrecy and false dawns.** Three nuclear powers pursue fusion in classified programs. Two announce breakthroughs that turn out to be wrong.
3. **1958–1990 — The [[tokamak]] era.** A Soviet design wins the world over, and performance climbs by six orders of magnitude.
4. **1991–2010 — Deuterium-tritium and the turn to [[iter|ITER]].** Real fusion power is produced, and the field bets everything on one enormous machine.
5. **2010–2021 — The quiet decade and the HTS revolution.** ITER stalls; a new superconductor changes the economics; private capital arrives.
6. **2022–2026 — [[ignition|Ignition]] and commercialisation.** Breakeven is achieved and the field reorganises around building plants.

## Era 1: Astrophysics (1920–1945)

**1920 — Aston, and then Eddington.**
Francis Aston's mass spectrograph showed that four hydrogen nuclei weigh about 0.7% more than one helium nucleus. Arthur Eddington immediately drew the conclusion in his 1920 address to the British Association: the Sun is powered by the conversion of hydrogen into helium, and *"if, indeed, the subatomic energy in the stars is being freely used to maintain their great furnaces, it seems to bring a little nearer to fulfilment our dream of controlling this latent power for the well-being of the human race."*

That is, as far as anyone can tell, the first statement of the fusion energy ambition — made 106 years before this document.

**1928 — Gamow's tunnelling.**
George Gamow's theory of alpha decay by quantum tunnelling supplied the missing mechanism. Classical physics said the Sun was far too cold to fuse hydrogen; tunnelling said it did not have to be hot enough to climb the barrier, only hot enough to tunnel through it.

**1929 — Atkinson and Houtermans** applied Gamow's result to stellar interiors and showed the numbers worked.

**1932 — the first fusion in a laboratory.**
Mark Oliphant, working with Paul Harteck under Rutherford at the Cavendish, accelerated deuterons into deuterium targets and observed the D-D reactions. In the process they **discovered tritium and helium-3**. This is the first deliberate fusion reaction produced by humans — and it consumed vastly more energy than it released, a property it has retained in most experiments since.

**1939 — Bethe closes the theory.**
Hans Bethe published the detailed reaction networks powering stars: the **proton-proton chain** for stars like the Sun, and the **CNO cycle** for hotter stars. He received the Nobel Prize for it in 1967. After 1939 there was no serious remaining question about *whether* fusion powers stars, only about how to reproduce it.

**1940s — the pinch.**
Independently, researchers noticed that a current flowing through a plasma generates its own magnetic field, which compresses ("pinches") the plasma column. In 1946 George Thomson and Moses Blackman filed a British patent for a fusion reactor based on the toroidal pinch. Peter Thonemann pursued the idea at Oxford and later Harwell. The pinch would dominate the first decade of fusion research and disappoint everybody.

## Era 2: Secrecy and false dawns (1946–1958)

Three countries ran classified fusion programs in parallel, each unaware of the others' details: the United States (**Project Sherwood**, from about 1951), the United Kingdom (Harwell and Aldermaston), and the Soviet Union (Kurchatov Institute).

**1951 — the Huemul affair.**
In March 1951, Argentine president Juan Perón announced that Ronald Richter, a German émigré scientist working on Huemul Island, had achieved controlled thermonuclear fusion. It was false — Richter's results were an artefact and the project collapsed under investigation within two years. But the announcement caused genuine alarm in Washington and is widely credited with accelerating US funding for fusion research.

**1951 — Spitzer invents the [[stellarator]].**
Reportedly prompted by the Huemul news, Lyman Spitzer Jr. at Princeton conceived a device in which the confining magnetic field is twisted by external coils rather than by a plasma current — a **stellarator**. The classified program he founded was called **Project Matterhorn**; it later became the [[pppl|Princeton Plasma Physics Laboratory]]. Martin Kruskal, recruited in 1951, would go on to co-discover the Kruskal-Shafranov stability limit.

Spitzer's key insight was that a simple toroidal field cannot confine a plasma — particle drifts carry it to the wall — and that the field lines must be given a rotational transform. The stellarator does this with 3D-shaped external coils; the tokamak would do it with a plasma current.

**1 November 1952 — Ivy Mike.**
The United States detonated the first true thermonuclear device at Enewetak Atoll: 10.4 megatons, using liquid deuterium cooled by a cryogenic plant weighing 82 tonnes. Fusion energy had been released on Earth. The problem of doing so gradually remained entirely open.

**1955 — Lawson writes the rules.**
John D. Lawson, at Harwell, produced a short, classified analysis of the minimum conditions for a fusion reactor to produce net power. It was declassified and published in 1957 and has governed the field ever since. Lawson himself framed it modestly: criteria for a *useful* thermonuclear reactor, which he took to mean gain of about 2.

**1955 — Geneva, and the 20-year prediction.**
At the first Atoms for Peace conference, Homi Bhabha predicted in his presidential address that a method of controlled fusion would be found "within the next two decades." This is the origin of the joke — *fusion is thirty years away and always will be* — which has been directionally accurate for seventy years and is now, for the first time, under genuine challenge.

**1956 — Kurchatov breaks ranks.**
Igor Kurchatov gave a lecture at Harwell describing Soviet fusion research, including the frank admission that neutrons observed in Soviet pinch experiments were **not thermonuclear** in origin. It was an extraordinary act of scientific transparency from a closed program, and it should have served as a warning.

**January 1958 — [[zeta|ZETA]].**
The Zero Energy Thermonuclear Assembly at Harwell was, when built, the largest and most powerful fusion device in the world. On 24 January 1958 the UKAEA announced that ZETA had produced neutrons of thermonuclear origin, with press coverage suggesting Britain had beaten the world to fusion power.

It was wrong. By May 1958 the team had established that the neutrons came from **beam-target reactions** driven by plasma instabilities accelerating ions, not from a thermal plasma at fusion temperatures. The retraction was public and painful.

**The ZETA fiasco had one enormously positive consequence.** It demonstrated to all three programs that claims could not be verified in secrecy and that everyone was making the same mistakes independently. At the **second Geneva conference in September 1958**, fusion research was comprehensively declassified. From that point on, fusion has been one of the most open fields in physics — a legacy of a mistake.

**1958 — the first tokamak.**
Building on ideas from Igor Tamm and Andrei Sakharov (who had sketched the concept in 1950–51), Natan Yavlinsky built **T-1** at the Kurchatov Institute. The name is a Russian acronym: *toroidal chamber with magnetic coils*. Its distinguishing feature relative to the pinch was a strong toroidal field — much stronger than the poloidal field from the plasma current — which stabilised the column against the instabilities that had wrecked ZETA.

## Era 3: The tokamak era (1958–1990)

**1961–1972 — lasers enter.**
Almost as soon as the laser was invented, Nikolai Basov in the USSR and others proposed using it to compress fusion fuel. In 1972 John Nuckolls and colleagues at Livermore published the foundational analysis of **laser-driven [[inertial-confinement|inertial confinement]] fusion** in *Nature*, predicting that a megajoule-scale laser could achieve high gain. The estimate was optimistic by roughly an order of magnitude in driver energy, but the concept was correct — it took fifty years and NIF to prove it.

**August 1968 — Novosibirsk.**
At the IAEA conference in Novosibirsk, the Soviet delegation reported that **[[t-3|T-3]]** had achieved electron temperatures of about 1 keV and confinement times an order of magnitude better than any Western device. The claim was received with considerable scepticism — memories of ZETA were fresh.

**1969 — the Culham expedition.**
In a genuinely remarkable episode of Cold War scientific cooperation, the Soviets invited a British team from Culham to bring their newly developed **laser Thomson scattering** diagnostic to Moscow and measure T-3 themselves. The team, led by Nicol Peacock and Derek Robinson, did exactly that. The Soviet results were confirmed — the electron temperature was real.

The effect was immediate and total. Within about two years, Princeton had converted its Model C stellarator into a tokamak (ST), and essentially every major fusion laboratory in the world reoriented around the tokamak. Fusion historians call it the **"great tokamak stampede."** The stellarator would spend the next forty years in the wilderness.

**1970s — the climb.**
A generation of tokamaks — ST and PLT at Princeton, ORMAK at Oak Ridge, Alcator at MIT, TFR in France, ASDEX in Germany, T-10 in the USSR — pushed performance up rapidly.
- **1978**: PLT achieved ion temperatures of 60 million K (well above the 40 million K needed for D-T) using [[neutral-beam-injection|neutral beam injection]], demonstrating that auxiliary heating could push a plasma far past ohmic limits.
- MIT's **Alcator** series pioneered the high-field, compact approach that would eventually become the intellectual ancestor of [[sparc|SPARC]], achieving record densities and confinement in small machines with very strong fields.

**1982 — [[h-mode|H-mode]].**
Fritz Wagner and colleagues on **ASDEX** in Germany discovered that above a threshold heating power, the plasma abruptly transitioned into a state with roughly **twice** the energy confinement time. The **high-confinement mode** was initially met with disbelief and then reproduced everywhere. It remains the baseline operating scenario for ITER and essentially every reactor design on the drawing board, and its discovery is arguably the single most valuable empirical result in magnetic fusion.

**25 June 1983 — [[jet|JET]] first plasma.**
The **Joint European Torus** at Culham, the largest tokamak ever built, achieved first plasma ahead of schedule and under budget. It would run for forty years and remain the world's most capable D-T facility until its retirement.

**1985 — Geneva, and the birth of ITER.**
At the Geneva superpower summit, Mikhail Gorbachev proposed to Ronald Reagan a joint international project to develop fusion energy — an idea reportedly seeded by Evgeny Velikhov. The proposal became **ITER**, with conceptual design activities beginning in 1988 under IAEA auspices, involving the USSR, USA, Japan and Euratom. Fusion thereby became a permanent instrument of scientific diplomacy, with all the funding stability and all the schedule dysfunction that implies.

**1989 — cold fusion.**
Martin Fleischmann and Stanley Pons announced at a press conference that they had achieved fusion in a palladium electrode at room temperature. The claim collapsed within months under failed replication. Its main legacy for mainstream fusion was reputational damage and a lasting institutional wariness about announcing results before peer review — a wariness visible in how carefully NIF and CFS handle their announcements today.

## Era 4: Deuterium-tritium, and betting on ITER (1991–2010)

Everything before 1991 was done with hydrogen or deuterium — safe, non-radioactive, and producing negligible fusion power. Actually using tritium meant accepting radioactivity, neutron activation of the machine, and remote handling forever after. Only three magnetic machines have ever done it.

**9 November 1991 — JET's Preliminary Tritium Experiment.**
JET introduced a small amount of tritium and produced about **1.7 MW** of fusion power for two seconds — the first controlled release of significant fusion power in a magnetic device.

**1993–1997 — [[tftr|TFTR]].**
Princeton's Tokamak Fusion Test Reactor ran the first extensive campaign with a **50/50 D-T mix**. It set a fusion power record of **10.7 MW in 1994** and produced **1,500 MJ** of total fusion energy across the campaign. TFTR was designated a Nuclear Historic Landmark in 2020. It shut down in 1997.

**1997 — JET DTE1.**
JET produced **16.1 MW** of peak fusion power for about a second, and **22 MJ** in a single pulse, at **Q_sci ≈ 0.67**. That record for fusion power stood for 25 years.

*(A definitional caution: 0.67 is the widely-cited **transient peak**. Wurzel and Hsu's harmonised dataset assigns the same shot a sustained Q_sci of **0.27**. Both are correct; they measure different things. See [[26-appendix-quantitative-data|Document 26]].)*

**1998 — the US walks out of ITER.**
Congress withdrew US participation amid concerns about cost and design. The remaining partners redesigned ITER at roughly half the fusion power (500 MW rather than 1,500 MW). The US rejoined in 2003. China and South Korea joined in 2003, India in 2005.

**28 June 2005 — site selection.** After a protracted contest between France and Japan, **Cadarache** in Provence was chosen. Japan received compensating commitments including the **[[jt-60sa|JT-60SA]]** project, the Broader Approach agreements, and IFMIF/EVEDA.

**21 November 2006 — the ITER Agreement** was signed at the Élysée Palace by seven Members representing 35 nations.

**2009 — NIF completes.**
The **[[nif|National Ignition Facility]]** at Livermore was declared complete in March 2009: 192 laser beams, frequency-tripled to 351 nm, capable of delivering roughly 1.8–2.2 MJ in a shaped nanosecond pulse. Its principal mission was stockpile stewardship — validating weapons physics without testing — with ignition as both a scientific goal and a stewardship requirement.

**2010–2012 — the National Ignition Campaign fails.**
NIF's formal campaign to achieve ignition ended in September 2012 without it. Yields were roughly an order of magnitude below predictions. The subsequent decade was spent understanding why: hydrodynamic instabilities seeded by the capsule fill tube and surface defects, hohlraum asymmetry, and laser-plasma instabilities. The lesson was that ICF is dominated not by the idealised physics but by **imperfections**.

## Era 5: The quiet decade and the HTS revolution (2010–2021)

Publicly, this was fusion's least glamorous period: NIF had missed, ITER's schedule and budget were deteriorating badly, and the field's credibility was thin. Underneath, three things happened that reset everything.

**First: stellarators came back.**
Computational optimisation made it possible to design 3D magnetic fields with good confinement properties — something impossible when Spitzer's design was abandoned. **[[wendelstein-7-x|Wendelstein 7-X]]** in Greifswald, the product of a decade of optimisation and construction, achieved first plasma (helium) on **10 December 2015** and hydrogen plasma in February 2016. Its 50 superconducting coils are shaped to a tolerance of about 1 mm and were, at the time, among the most complex engineered objects ever built.

**Second: REBCO tape became a commercial product.**
Rare-earth barium copper oxide high-temperature superconductor, developed for the power industry, turned out to sustain very high current densities in very high magnetic fields — well beyond the ~12–13 T ceiling of the Nb₃Sn used in ITER. Since **fusion power density scales as B⁴**, this was not an incremental improvement. It meant a machine with twice the field could do the same job at a fraction of the volume — and therefore, potentially, a fraction of the cost and construction time.

**Third: private capital arrived.**
[[tae-technologies|TAE Technologies]] had been founded in 1998 and [[general-fusion|General Fusion]] in 2002, but the modern wave began around 2015–2018. **[[commonwealth-fusion-systems|Commonwealth Fusion Systems]]** spun out of MIT's Plasma Science and Fusion Center in 2018 explicitly to exploit HTS, with the SPARC/[[arc|ARC]] design lineage developed by [[dennis-whyte|Dennis Whyte]], Martin Greenwald, Bob Mumgaard and colleagues.

**5 September 2021 — the 20 tesla magnet.**
CFS and MIT tested a large-bore REBCO toroidal-field model coil to **20 T**. It is difficult to overstate how consequential this single test was for the sector: it converted the high-field compact tokamak from a paper argument into an engineering fact, and it directly precipitated CFS's $1.8 billion Series B — at the time the largest private fusion raise ever — and the wave of investment that followed.

**8 August 2021 — NIF reaches the threshold.**
Shot N210808 produced **1.35 MJ** from 1.9 MJ of laser energy — a gain of about 0.7, and, importantly, the first plasma in which alpha self-heating dominated. It met most definitions of a **[[burning-plasma|burning plasma]]** and reached the threshold of ignition without crossing it.

## Era 6: Ignition and commercialisation (2022–2026)

**5 December 2022 — ignition.**
NIF shot N221204 delivered 2.05 MJ of laser energy to the target and produced **3.15 MJ** of fusion energy: **target gain 1.5**. For the first time in history, a controlled fusion reaction released more energy than was delivered to it. The [[us-doe|US Department of Energy]] announced it on 13 December 2022.

The caveats are real and were stated at the time — the laser drew hundreds of megajoules from the grid to deliver 2.05 MJ — but the scientific threshold was genuine and had stood unbroken since Lawson wrote it down in 1955.

**3 October 2023 — JET's farewell.**
In its final deuterium-tritium campaign (**DTE3**, 31 August to 14 October 2023), JET produced **69 MJ** of fusion energy over about five seconds from **0.2 milligrams** of fuel — a new record for total fusion energy from a magnetic device. The campaign also established two advanced exhaust regimes, **quasi-continuous exhaust** and the **X-point radiator**, first in deuterium and then in D-T — directly relevant to ITER's [[divertor]] problem.

JET ceased plasma operations at the end of December 2023 after forty years. Within a year, 96 research papers had been submitted from the final campaigns. Its decommissioning and repurposing program, itself a first-of-a-kind exercise in dismantling a tritium-contaminated tokamak, will run to roughly 2040.

**October 2023 — JT-60SA first plasma.** The Japan-EU superconducting tokamak, the largest in operation, achieved first plasma.

**2024 — the ITER rebaseline.**
At its June 2024 meeting the ITER Council received a comprehensively revised schedule. The old staged plan — "First Plasma 2025" with a stripped-down machine — was replaced with **Start of Research Operation in 2034** using a far more complete machine, followed by 27 months of research. Full magnetic energy moved to 2036, D-D operation to 2035, and **Start of D-T Operation to 2039**, four years later than the previous baseline. Painful, but widely regarded within the field as more honest and technically better sequenced than what it replaced.

**15 July 2024 — [[wham|WHAM]]'s 17 tesla.** The Wisconsin HTS Axisymmetric Mirror at UW-Madison confined a plasma at **17 T** — the highest magnetic field ever applied to a confined fusion plasma — reviving the [[magnetic-mirror|magnetic mirror]] concept that had been cancelled in the US in 1986.

**June 2024 — [[hh70|HH70]].** Shanghai's [[energy-singularity|Energy Singularity]] operated **HH70**, the world's first **fully high-temperature-superconducting tokamak**, built in roughly two years. China had entered the private fusion race.

**January 2025 — [[east|EAST]] holds for 1,066 seconds.** China's Experimental Advanced Superconducting Tokamak sustained a high-confinement plasma for **1,066 seconds** — over 17 minutes — beating its own 403-second record from 2023 and crossing the symbolically important 1,000-second mark.

**7 April 2025 — 8.6 megajoules.** NIF's eighth ignition shot produced **8.6 MJ** from 2.08 MJ: **target gain 4.13**. The enabling change was reported to be a higher-quality high-density-carbon (diamond) capsule rather than any change to the laser — confirming that ICF performance is limited by target perfection.

**2025 — Wendelstein 7-X takes the triple-product record.** In a campaign ending 22 May, W7-X set a world record [[triple-product|triple product]] for plasma durations above 10 seconds, sustained across a 43-second discharge — beating JET's long-pulse record with a plasma **three times smaller** and **five times less heating power**. For a stellarator to beat a tokamak on any confinement figure of merit was, historically, unthinkable.

**October 2025 / June 2026 — the US writes it down.** DOE published Part I of its **Fusion Science and Technology Roadmap** in October 2025 and Part II in June 2026, with input from over 800 scientists and engineers across 15+ private companies, 10+ national labs and 70+ universities. It committed the US to a **Build-Innovate-Grow** strategy targeting commercialisation by the mid-2030s.

**26 February 2026 — the NRC proposes a rule.** Fusion machines would be regulated under the **byproduct material framework** rather than as reactors — the single most important regulatory decision in the field's history, and one that materially reduces the licensing burden on every US developer. Final rule targeted October 2026.

**2026 — the year the industry became an industry.**

- **February**: Helion's **[[polaris|Polaris]]** hits 150 million °C and becomes the first privately funded machine to operate with D-T fuel.
- **March**: UKAEA takes ownership of the West Burton site for **[[step|STEP]]**, three and a half years ahead of schedule.
- **April**: CFS begins commercialising its magnets as a separate business line, starting with [[realta-fusion|Realta Fusion]]. [[tokamak-energy|Tokamak Energy]]'s **Demo4** reaches 11.8 T in a full tokamak-configuration HTS magnet.
- **June**: **Realta demonstrates [[direct-energy-conversion|direct energy conversion]]** of plasma energy to electricity — a first for a commercial fusion company. [[pacific-fusion|Pacific Fusion]]'s scaled pulser module delivers **>440 GW peak** at ~1.1 MV. NIF ignites for the **eleventh time** (7.9 MJ, gain ~3.8).
- **July**: **General Fusion lists on Nasdaq as GFUZ** — the first publicly traded fusion company. CFS raises **$1 billion from pension funds**. The FIA reports **$4.48 bn raised in a year, $14.24 bn cumulative**.
- **August**: SPARC passes **80% assembly**; all 18 toroidal field magnets due in place by summer's end. Inertia and LLNL cut cryogenic D-T fuel layering from days to **2–3 hours**. DOE approves Xcimer's power-plant architecture. ORNL licenses cryogenic pellet fuelling to [[type-one-energy|Type One Energy]] and stands up **[[unity-3|UNITY-3]]**, the first facility producing 14 MeV neutrons to test commercial breeding blankets.

## The performance climb, in one table

| Year | Machine | Achievement | Q_sci or gain |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1932 | Cavendish | First lab fusion (D-D) | ~10⁻⁹ |
| 1958 | ZETA | Claimed thermonuclear neutrons — **retracted** | — |
| 1968 | T-3 | ~1 keV electron temperature | ~10⁻⁵ |
| 1978 | PLT | 60 million K ion temperature | — |
| 1982 | ASDEX | H-mode discovered | — |
| 1991 | JET (PTE) | 1.7 MW fusion power | ~0.15 |
| 1994 | TFTR | 10.7 MW fusion power | ~0.27 |
| 1997 | JET (DTE1) | 16.1 MW peak, 22 MJ | **0.67** |
| 2021 | NIF (N210808) | 1.35 MJ, burning plasma | 0.72 |
| **2022** | **NIF (N221204)** | **3.15 MJ — first ignition** | **1.5** |
| 2023 | JET (DTE3) | 69 MJ over 5 s | **0.37** |
| 2025 | NIF | 8.6 MJ | **4.13** |
| 2026 | NIF | 7.9 MJ, 11th ignition | ~3.8 |
| **2027** | **SPARC (planned)** | **First magnetic-confinement Q > 1** | **>1 (target)** |
| 2039 | ITER (planned) | 500 MW burning plasma | 10 (target) |

Measured by triple product, magnetic fusion improved by roughly **six orders of magnitude between 1960 and 2000** — a rate comparable to Moore's law over the same period. It then largely plateaued for two decades, because the machines that would have continued the climb were not built: the field's resources went into ITER, and ITER was late.

The current era's distinguishing feature is that **the climb has resumed on multiple independent tracks at once** — ICF gain at NIF, pulse length in China, triple product in stellarators, and field strength in HTS magnets — and, for the first time, most of the money driving it is private.

## What the history should teach us

**1. Fusion's failures have been failures of diagnosis, not of ambition.** ZETA was not a fraud; it was a measurement problem. The National Ignition Campaign did not fail because ignition was impossible; it failed because 3D imperfections mattered more than 1D physics predicted. In both cases the fix was better instruments and better simulation, and in both cases it eventually worked.

**2. Openness has been unusually productive.** The 1958 declassification, the 1969 Culham expedition to Moscow, and the ITER agreement all made fusion faster. The current partial re-closure — Chinese companies declining to report to the FIA survey, export-controlled REBCO supply chains — is a genuine departure from that tradition.

**3. The "always thirty years away" joke was true, and its truth had a cause.** Fusion timelines slipped mainly because funding slipped. The 1976 US ERDA plan showed that with aggressive funding a demonstration plant was achievable by roughly 1990; actual appropriations tracked the plan's lowest "fusion never" curve almost exactly. The current era is the first in which the resources approximately match the ambition — and notably, it is private money that closed the gap.

**4. Concepts return.** The stellarator was abandoned in 1969 and leads the world in triple product at long pulse in 2026. The magnetic mirror was cancelled in 1986 and set a field record in 2024. Nothing in fusion is permanently dead; it is usually waiting for an enabling technology — computation, superconductors, or lasers.
