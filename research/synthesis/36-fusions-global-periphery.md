# 36 — Fusion's Global Periphery

*Project Lux · The forty countries that are not the big eight*

---

## 1. Why the periphery matters

[[08-national-programs|Document 08]] covered the eight programmes that dominate fusion by expenditure: the US, EU, UK, China, Japan, Korea, India and Russia. That accounting is correct on money and misleading on participation.

**There are roughly 90 fusion devices operating worldwide, of which about 41 are tokamaks.** Most are small, most are in universities, and a substantial number are in countries with no national fusion programme at all. They train the workforce, they produce a large share of the field's published physics, and — as the workforce analysis in [[27-people-institutions-public-trust|Document 27]] shows — they are the pipeline that the commercial sector will drain.

The periphery also determines something the big-eight framing misses entirely: **whether fusion becomes a technology a dozen countries have or one a hundred countries have.** That is a question about capacity building, not about magnets.

## 2. The notable national efforts outside the big eight

### Kazakhstan — [[ktm|KTM]]

The **Kazakhstan [[tokamak|Tokamak]] for Material Testing** is one of the more strategically interesting small devices in the world, because it was designed for a purpose rather than as a general-purpose physics machine.

KTM is built to **model plasma-material interactions in the [[divertor]] region under conditions expected for [[iter|ITER]]**. Its designers claim conditions inside KTM are as close as currently possible to ITER's divertor environment, which — if accurate — makes it a genuinely useful materials testbed rather than a prestige project.

The context is worth stating. Kazakhstan hosted the Soviet Union's principal nuclear weapons test site at Semipalatinsk, with all the consequences that implies for its population and land. A country that was on the receiving end of nuclear technology building a facility to help develop its cleanest form is not merely symbolic — it is also a rational use of an existing nuclear-technical workforce and infrastructure.

Kazakhstan also participates in ITER through Russia.

### Brazil — [[tcabr|TCABR]] and the Southern Hemisphere

**Brazil is the only country in the Southern Hemisphere with operating fusion devices**, and it has three small tokamaks.

The principal one is **TCABR** — *Tokamak Chauffage Alfvén Brésilien* — at the **University of São Paulo**, the **largest tokamak in the southern hemisphere**. Its history is a good illustration of how the periphery actually acquires capability: it was **originally built by EPFL in Switzerland**, where it operated from **1980 to 1992**, then **reassembled in Brazil and operating since 1999**, with periodic upgrades.

A second-hand European machine, given a second thirty-year life on another continent, training two generations of plasma physicists. That is the model by which fusion capability spreads, and it is far cheaper than building new.

Brazil has established nuclear and plasma research communities and, in the IEA/IAEA framing, opportunities to expand technical training.

### Australia

Australia contributes strengths in **plasma science, engineering research and international collaboration** rather than through a large domestic device. Its notable entries:

- **[[hb11-energy|HB11 Energy]]** (Sydney) — the proton-boron-11 laser fusion company, which reported a roughly tenfold increase in measured alpha yield at ELI Beamlines in May 2026 ([[03-confinement-approaches|Document 03]])
- Plans for a **laser-driven** device rather than a magnetic one — a sensible choice for a country with strong optics and laser physics but no tokamak tradition
- **HostPlus**, an Australian pension fund, appears in the FIA's 2026 investor list — part of the institutional shift documented in [[34-investment-mechanics|Document 34]]

### The small-nation companies

Several countries with no national fusion programme have a company in the FIA's 2026 survey:

**OpenStar deserves particular note.** A company in Wellington, New Zealand achieved plasma in a levitated dipole — a configuration nobody had commercialised — in 2024, having been founded in 2021. It is the clearest evidence that fusion's entry cost has fallen enough for a small-nation startup to produce a real result.

### Others with real activity
**Italy** — ENEA's **DTT** at Frascati is the largest magnetic confinement facility under construction in Europe after ITER, dedicated to divertor and alternative-configuration research ([[16-heating-current-drive-fuelling|Document 16]]); Consorzio RFX in Padua hosts **SPIDER and MITICA**, ITER's neutral beam test facility, plus RFX-mod2. Italy is a major fusion nation that the "big eight" framing understates.
**Spain** — **[[ifmif-dones|IFMIF-DONES]]** at Granada, plus the TJ-II [[stellarator]] at CIEMAT.
**Netherlands** — DIFFER, plasma-material interaction.
**Portugal, Belgium, Poland, Czechia, Austria, Finland, Denmark, Romania, Greece** — all [[eurofusion|EUROfusion]] members with device access and national contributions. **ELI Beamlines** in Czechia hosts petawatt laser research used by HB11.
**Thailand** — see below.

## 3. How capability actually spreads: the IAEA's small-device network

This is the mechanism, and it is almost entirely unreported.

**Since 2004 the IAEA has run a coordinated research project on small and medium size fusion devices, bringing together researchers from 19 countries.** A recent five-year CRP linked **24 institutions in 18 countries**, comprising 17 research contracts, six research agreements and one technical contract. A new project to strengthen the global small and medium device network has been launched.

The logic is that a small tokamak costs a few million dollars, trains PhD students, produces publishable physics on edge turbulence and diagnostics, and gives a country a seat at the table. It is the cheapest possible entry point into a field whose flagship machines cost tens of billions.

### Regional programmes

**Southeast Asia.** The **ASEAN School on Plasma and Nuclear Fusion** has run since 2014 and has been a key activity in **initiating a fusion programme in Thailand** and advancing regional research. In January 2020 the IAEA and ITER supported the school, with **over 80 young researchers from the region** participating.

**Latin America.** **Costa Rica** receives IAEA support to close technical gaps through capacity building. A 2019 workshop at the Technology Institute of Costa Rica gave participants from across Latin America the chance to take part in **joint experiments and receive training operating small fusion devices**.

**Africa.** The honest position: **Africa currently lacks a structured framework for fusion research, education and workforce development.** There is no equivalent of the ASEAN school, no regional device network, and no African country in the FIA's 56.

That is a gap with consequences beyond fairness. Africa's electricity demand will grow more than any other region's over the period in which fusion might become deployable, and per the climate modelling in [[35-fusion-and-climate|Document 35]], regions with high demand growth are precisely where firm generation is most valuable. **A technology developed entirely without the participation of the continent that will need it most is a predictable source of later friction** — over technology transfer, over cost, and over who is permitted to have it.

The IAEA's **Technical Meeting on Developing the Fusion Workforce** (27–30 April 2026) is the relevant convening point, and the workforce crisis documented in [[27-people-institutions-public-trust|Document 27]] gives the developed programmes a self-interested reason to care: **the countries with the fastest-growing populations of STEM graduates are the ones not currently in the network.**

## 4. The maritime entrants

A market segment that emerged in 2025–26 and does not fit the national framing, because shipping is inherently transnational.

**The problem:** ships carrying roughly **90% of world trade** burn some of the dirtiest fuel in use. Maritime decarbonisation is genuinely hard — batteries lack the energy density for ocean crossings, ammonia and hydrogen require enormous tank volumes that displace cargo, and biofuels do not scale.

### [[maritime-fusion|Maritime Fusion]]
Founded **2024**, headquartered in San Francisco, **founded by SpaceX and Tesla engineers**, a Y Combinator W25 company.

It is developing a **complete ship-based propulsion system** — tokamak, shielding, cryogenic plant, magnet power supplies, tritium handling, heat conversion and supporting systems — with the argument that the whole assembly would be **far smaller than the fuel tanks required for an ammonia- or hydrogen-powered vessel**. That is the key economic claim: on a ship, volume is revenue, and a compact energy source that displaces less cargo can command a premium a land-based plant cannot.

**Target: first deployment 2032.**

### FusPoB — the Fusion Power Barge
A different and more conservative approach: a **Joint Industry Project** assessing the feasibility of installing and operating a compact fusion reactor aboard a vessel.

The consortium is notable for who is in it: **nT-Tao** (Israel, the reactor), **ABS** (a major ship classification society — i.e. the body that would have to certify it), **Siemens Energy**, **P&P Marine Consultants**, and **TEMISTh**.

The concept: a **containerised fusion reactor generating up to 20 MWe**, installed in a **71.4-metre barge**, driving **two 8,000 kWe steam generators**, with **battery backup providing six hours of autonomy at eight knots**. Commercial prototype targeted for roughly **2032**.

**The presence of a classification society in the consortium is the significant detail.** ABS's involvement means the project is being assessed against actual maritime certification requirements from the start, rather than as a paper study — which is the maritime equivalent of the regulatory clarity that the NRC rule gave land-based developers ([[11-policy-regulation-safety|Document 11]]).

**Assessment:** both are extremely early and both target 2032, which on the evidence of every other fusion timeline in this dossier should be read as "the 2030s at the earliest." But the market logic is sound — shipping has no good decarbonisation option, values volumetric energy density above almost everything, already operates nuclear vessels (naval, and a handful of civilian), and pays for fuel at sea-freight rather than grid prices.

## 5. Assessment

**Fusion is more globally distributed than the funding numbers suggest.** Ninety devices, forty-one tokamaks, participants across dozens of countries, and an IAEA network deliberately keeping small-device research alive as a training and entry mechanism.

**It is also more concentrated than it has ever been.** The FIA's 56 companies sit in 13 countries; 28 are in the United States; all five billion-dollar companies are American. As fusion moves from a science programme to an industry, the periphery risks being relegated from *participant* to *customer* — a transition the field has not discussed and the IAEA is, more or less alone, trying to manage.

**The absence of Africa is the most conspicuous gap**, and it is a gap in the region whose electricity demand will grow most over exactly the period fusion might become deployable.

**The maritime entrants are worth watching** less for their 2032 dates than for what they represent: fusion companies forming around a *market requirement* — volumetric energy density at sea — rather than around a confinement concept. That is what an industry looks like when it starts being pulled by demand instead of pushed by physics.
