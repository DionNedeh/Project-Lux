# 08 — National Programs

*Project Lux · Who is spending what, on which strategy, and why the geopolitics has changed*

---

## Overview: the strategic reframing of 2024–2026

Until roughly 2023, national fusion programs were **science programs**. They funded universities and national laboratories to answer physics questions, with [[iter|ITER]] as the shared destination and a demonstration power plant somewhere beyond it.

Between 2024 and 2026, essentially every major program rewrote itself as an **industrial policy**. The language changed from "research roadmap" to "commercialisation strategy"; the timelines moved from the 2050s to the 2030s and 2040s; and the instruments shifted from grants to public-private partnerships, cost-share milestone programs, and infrastructure that private developers can rent.

Three things caused this: the private sector's arrival with more money than any government fusion budget; NIF's [[ignition]], which removed the "will it ever work" objection; and AI-driven electricity demand, which gave fusion an identified customer for the first time.

The FIA report's framing is worth quoting: *"while the headlines talk about a 'race' between countries like the United States and China, the ones running the race are the companies. What governments can do is build the infrastructure, provide the incentives, and ensure the supply chain, workforce, and regulations are able to support the growth."*

## United States

### Strategy: Build – Innovate – Grow

The **DOE Fusion Science and Technology Roadmap** (Part I October 2025, Part II June 2026) is the governing document. It was developed with input from **over 800 scientists and engineers**, including **15+ private companies, 10+ national laboratories and 70+ universities**, and it is explicit about its purpose: *"a comprehensive national strategy to accelerate the development and commercialization of fusion energy by the mid-2030s"* — Darío Gil, Under Secretary for Science.

The three pillars:

- **Build** — key infrastructure to close Fusion Materials and Technology gaps, plus an **AI-Fusion Digital Convergence Platform**
- **Innovate** — advance the science and engineering with defined milestones and metrics, via the **FIRE Collaboratives** ($107 M announced)
- **Grow** — expand the ecosystem through public-private partnerships, regional consortia, supply chains, and workforce

### The six Core Challenge Areas

| | Area |
|---|---|
| **A** | Structural Materials Science and Technology |
| **B** | Plasma-Facing Components and Plasma-Material Interactions |
| **C** | Advancing Confinement Approaches |
| **D** | Fuel Cycle and Tritium Processing |
| **E** | Blanket Science and Technology |
| **F** | Fusion Plant Engineering and System Integration |

Each is tracked with near-term (2–3 yr), mid-term (3–5 yr) and long-term (5–10 yr) milestones. Selected concrete targets:

- **Milestone 1: scientific Q > 1** — first demonstration in a US fusion device *(i.e. SPARC)*
- **SPARC to demonstrate Q > 1 and progress toward Q > 5** with alpha-dominated plasmas (mid-term)
- **TBR > 1.1** as the FPP-scale fuel-cycle target; long-term **TRL 7 integrated blanket at TBR > 1.1**
- Irradiation datasets to **~10 dpa** for Fe-alloys and V-alloys (mid-term)
- Domestic industrial heats of **multi-tonne Fe-alloy and 500 kg V-alloy**
- Codify fusion-specific requirements into **ASME-based standards**
- IFE: high-rep-rate drivers, domestic single-beamline demonstrators, access to a **multi-petawatt laser facility**

### Eight Infrastructure Streams

1. Plasma confinement and performance
2. Enabling technologies development and testing
3. Exhaust and plasma / high-heat-flux testing
4. **Nuclear-effects testing** (fusion-prototypic neutrons and hot cells)
5. Remote maintenance and balance-of-plant
6. **Blanket development and testing**
7. **Fuel-cycle development and testing**
8. HPC, advanced manufacturing and AI

The pathway: **near term (2–3 yr)** neutron-source surrogates and non-nuclear blanket test stands; **mid term (3–5 yr)** construct the **Fusion Prototypical Neutron Source (FPNS)**; **long term (5–10 yr)** integrate with private-sector pilot plant programs. DOE ties these explicitly to *company* schedules — an unusual and deliberate inversion of the normal relationship.

### Key programs and facilities

- **Milestone-Based Fusion Development Program** (May 2023, NASA-COTS-inspired): eight companies — **CFS, [[focused-energy|Focused Energy]], [[thea-energy|Thea Energy]] (as Princeton Stellarators), [[realta-fusion|Realta Fusion]], [[tokamak-energy|Tokamak Energy]], [[type-one-energy|Type One Energy]], [[xcimer-energy|Xcimer Energy]], [[zap-energy|Zap Energy]]**. Payment on demonstrated milestones. **Thea Energy became the first awardee to complete a full fusion pilot plant design review**; DOE approved **Xcimer's "Athena" preconceptual design** in August 2026.
- **INFUSE** — Innovation Network for Fusion Energy; connects companies to national lab capability. With the Milestone Program, the most-cited PPP in the FIA survey.
- **IFE-STAR** — $42 M across three inertial fusion energy hubs: Colorado State (RISE), University of Rochester, LLNL (STARFIRE).
- **[[mpex|MPEX]]** (ORNL) — Material Plasma Exposure eXperiment, for plasma-facing component testing.
- **[[unity-3|UNITY-3]]** (ORNL, 2026) — **the first facility generating 14 MeV neutrons to evaluate commercial breeding blanket designs and validate tritium production.** [[kyoto-fusioneering|Kyoto Fusioneering]] is relocating its US HQ to Oak Ridge alongside it.
- **[[diii-d|DIII-D]]**, **NSTX-U**, **NIF**, **[[z-machine|Z Machine]]**, **OMEGA** — the operating research base.
- **Genesis Mission** — the AI/HPC initiative the roadmap leverages for fusion modelling.

### Budget

**Fusion Energy Sciences received roughly $790–806 million in FY2026.** For scale, that is *less than one-third* of the capitalisation of the single state-owned company China created for fusion in July 2025.

### Regulation

The NRC's proposed rule of **26 February 2026** places fusion under the **byproduct material framework** rather than reactor licensing. Final rule targeted **October 2026**. See [[11-policy-regulation-safety|Document 11]] for the full treatment. This is arguably the highest-leverage thing the US government has done for fusion, and it cost nothing.

### Assessment

The US has the strongest private sector by a wide margin (28 of 56 companies, all five billion-dollar companies), a well-designed public program that has deliberately positioned itself as a *supplier* to that sector, and the best regulatory framework in the world. Its weakness is appropriations risk: the roadmap disclaims funding commitments on nearly every page — *"This Roadmap does not commit the Department of Energy to specific funding levels"* — and the infrastructure the private sector needs most (FPNS, blanket and fuel-cycle test facilities) is precisely the part that requires Congress to appropriate money for facilities that will not operate for a decade.

## China

### Scale and structure

China's fusion effort is the best-resourced in the world and the least transparent.

- **Estimated annual spending: ~$1.5 billion** — roughly twice the US federal fusion budget, per the head of DOE's own Office of Fusion Energy Sciences.
- **China Fusion Energy Co. Ltd (CFEC)** was established in Shanghai in **July 2025** under the **China National Nuclear Corporation**, with **~$2.1 billion (15 billion yuan) registered capital** — a single company capitalised at roughly **2.5× the entire annual US DOE fusion budget**.
- The **15th Five-Year Plan (2026–2030)** promises *"extraordinary measures"* to secure breakthroughs in fusion, designating it a frontline of great-power scientific competition.

### Facilities

- **[[east|EAST]]** (Hefei) — the long-pulse world leader. **1,066 s [[h-mode|H-mode]]** (January 2025). January 2026: reported confirmation of a **"density-free region"**, a possible route past the Greenwald limit.
- **HL-3** (Chengdu, SWIP) — advanced [[divertor]] and high-performance research; achieved 1 MA H-mode.
- **BEST** ([[burning-plasma|Burning plasma]] Experimental Superconducting Tokamak, Hefei) — **under construction**, intended to demonstrate burning plasma and, per Chinese statements, fusion power generation on a schedule aggressive relative to ITER.
- **CFETR** — the planned engineering test reactor / DEMO equivalent.
- **CRAFT** (Hefei) — a large fusion technology test complex for magnets, divertors and blankets, built *in parallel with* rather than after the plasma machines.
- **SG-III / SG-IV** — the laser [[inertial-confinement|inertial confinement]] program.

### Private sector

Four companies appear in the FIA survey; the FIA states plainly that more exist and declined to respond.

- **[[enn|ENN Science and Technology Development]]** — $720 m declared. Spherical torus, p-¹¹B ambitions.
- **[[energy-singularity|Energy Singularity]]** (Shanghai) — $112.5 m. **[[hh70|HH70]]: the world's first fully high-temperature-superconducting tokamak**, first plasma June 2024, built in roughly two years, and **1,337 s** steady-state long-pulse. **HH170** (Q ≥ 10 target) due to complete construction end-2027.
- **[[startorus|Startorus Fusion]]** (Xi'an) — ~$139 m declared, $220 m raised this year.
- **[[yan-fusion|YAN Fusion]] (Shanghai)** — $293 m (phased), a new entrant.
- **[[neofusion|NeoFusion]]** — also active.

### Assessment

China's distinguishing strategic feature is **industrial-supply-chain integration**. It manufactures its own REBCO tape, its own superconducting magnets, its own tungsten components, and its own cryogenics, at a scale no other country matches. Where the West's fusion supply chain is a constraint, China's is an asset — and the FIA and DOE roadmap both flag that Li-6 enrichment and HTS tape capacity are concentrated in China and Russia, which is a genuine sovereignty exposure for everyone else.

The counterargument: EAST and HH70's records are *duration* and *field* records in deuterium plasmas. **China has not run a D-T campaign, has not achieved Q > 1, and has no ignition.** Its programme is formidable in engineering throughput and has not yet demonstrated a physics result the West has not.

The honest summary is that China is building the *industrial* capability for fusion faster than anyone, while the US and Europe hold the lead in demonstrated plasma performance. Whether industrial capability or physics leadership matters more depends entirely on how hard the remaining physics turns out to be.

## European Union

### [[eurofusion|EUROfusion]] and the roadmap

**EUROfusion** is the coordinating consortium: **30 members across 29 countries**, funded through **Euratom**. Its *European Research Roadmap to the Realisation of Fusion Energy* has governed European fusion since 2012, structured around eight missions and targeting:

- All know-how in place to **start DEMO construction** in the 2030s
- **Fusion electricity on the European grid by 2050**

**DEMO** is the EU's demonstration power plant: a large conventional tokamak intended to generate several hundred MW of net electricity, breed its own tritium, and demonstrate the availability a utility requires. It is deliberately conservative — the EU's strategy has been to let ITER retire the physics risk and then build a machine that requires no new physics.

### The 2026 shift

The roadmap is being substantially revised. EUROfusion states the programme *"will move from being laboratory-based and science-driven towards an industry- and technology-driven venture"* — which is precisely the shift described at the top of this document.

- The **EU's first-ever Fusion Strategy** is due in 2026
- A **public-private partnership with the EU fusion industry** is being established
- Alignment is being made to the **ITER Baseline 2024** and to the Fusion Expert Group's guidance

### Key facilities

- **ITER** (Cadarache, France) — see [[06-iter-program|Document 06]]
- **[[wendelstein-7-x|Wendelstein 7-X]]** (Greifswald, Germany) — the world's leading [[stellarator]]; triple-product record for long pulses
- **[[asdex-upgrade|ASDEX Upgrade]]** (Garching), **[[tcv|TCV]]** (Lausanne), **[[west|WEST]]** (Cadarache), **RFX-mod2** (Padua), **LMJ** (France's NIF equivalent)
- **[[ifmif-dones|IFMIF-DONES]]** (Granada, Spain) — the fusion materials irradiation facility. **40 MeV, 125 mA deuterons on a lithium jet**, targeting ~20 dpa in phase 1 and ~50 dpa in phase 2. Europe's answer to the field's hardest problem.
- **Fusion for Energy (F4E)** — the EU's domestic agency for ITER, and the engine of European fusion industrial capability

## Germany

Germany has moved fastest of any European country and now has the most explicit national ambition in the world.

- **Fusion Action Plan** approved by cabinet, implementing a flagship measure of the **High-Tech Agenda Germany** (BMFTR, July 2025), which names fusion as one of six critical future technologies
- **More than €2 billion (US$2.3 bn) to be invested by 2029** in fusion research, new research infrastructure, and pilot projects
- Explicit stated goal: **build "the world's first fusion power plant" in Germany**, targeting the 2040s
- **Three new state-funded fusion hubs** — laser fusion, magnetic fusion, and a cross-cutting hub for fuel cycles and materials. **Funding begins August 2026.**
- A **Fusion Energy Research and Innovation Roadmap (FIRE)** due by end-2026 to identify the required technologies

Germany also hosts an unusually strong private cluster: **[[proxima-fusion|Proxima Fusion]]** ($752 m), **[[marvel-fusion|Marvel Fusion]]** ($440 m), **Focused Energy** (US/German, $500 m), and **[[gauss-fusion|Gauss Fusion]]** — with the FIA noting that both federal *and* state governments announced investments into partnerships to build fusion power plants. Combined with Max Planck IPP's W7-X and ASDEX Upgrade, Germany has the deepest fusion research base in Europe.

## United Kingdom

The UK left Euratom after Brexit and responded by building the most fully articulated national fusion delivery programme in the West.

### Money and strategy

- **UK Fusion Energy Strategy, March 2026** — *"A new energy revolution: the UK's plan for delivering fusion energy"*
- **Over £2.5 billion committed across five years** — a record UK fusion commitment
- **UKAEA Strategy 2026–2030** aligns the agency to it

### STEP — Spherical Tokamak for Energy Production

The flagship: a **prototype fusion power plant**, not an experiment, at **West Burton, Nottinghamshire** (a former coal-fired station), targeting net electricity in the 2040s. Delivered by **UK Industrial Fusion Solutions Ltd (UKIFS)**.

**2026 progress:**
- First land parcel transferred from EDF **23 March 2026 — three and a half years ahead of schedule**
- **£200 million** Construction Partner contract to the **ILIOS consortium** (Kier–NUVIA JV)
- **Tokamak Energy** selected as **Magnet Systems Partner (£70 m / $95 m)**
- Site capacity tripled to ~120 professionals; demolition and ash extraction ongoing
- First public consultation ran 14 January to 11 March 2026
- UKIFS published its first Annual Report and Accounts

### The supporting facilities — the UK's real differentiator

- **[[librti|LIBRTI]]** (Lithium Breeding Tritium Innovation) — **£220 m over 2025/26–2029/30**. A **global first-of-a-kind facility for tritium breeding technology.** Given that tritium self-sufficiency is the field's second-hardest problem, this is one of the most strategically well-chosen public investments anywhere.
- **[[h3at|H3AT]]** (Hydrogen-3 Advanced Technology) — UKAEA with **Eni**, for tritium processing and handling at plant scale.
- **[[jet|JET]] Decommissioning and Repurposing** — the world's first dismantling of a tritium-contaminated tokamak, generating irreplaceable end-of-life data. Runs to ~2040.
- **[[mast-upgrade|MAST-Upgrade]]** — the [[super-x-divertor|Super-X divertor]] testbed.
- **Culham AI Growth Zone** — £125 m, including **£45 m for "Sunrise"**, a 1.4 MW fusion-dedicated supercomputer.

### Assessment

The UK's programme is small in absolute terms but exceptionally well targeted. Rather than compete on machine size, it has invested in **the two things nobody else is adequately funding — tritium breeding and tritium handling** — plus a genuine prototype plant with real construction money and a site. It is also the only country to have converted a retired coal station into a fusion site ahead of schedule, which is a meaningful signal about permitting and local politics.

## Japan

### The strategic acceleration

Japan formally **revised its fusion power generation demonstration target from "around 2050" to the 2030s** — one of the largest timeline accelerations by any government, and a direct response to private-sector momentum. A Cabinet Office specialised team was established to revise the national fusion strategy, and the country is explicitly transitioning from research to commercialisation.

### Facilities and programmes

- **JT-60SA** (Naka) — the **largest superconducting tokamak in operation**. First plasma October 2023. Upgraded 2024–26 with a new carbon-armoured first wall, divertor and in-vessel control coils. Next campaign starts end-2026, running ~6 months.
- **LHD** (NIFS, Toki) — **operations concluded 25 December 2025** after 27 years and 200,000 shots. The world's second-largest superconducting stellarator.
- **Moonshot Programme** — the government's high-risk R&D vehicle, cited by companies in the FIA survey.
- **FAST** (Fusion by Advanced Superconducting Tokamak) — led by **Starlight Engine Ltd** with Kyoto Fusioneering, an industry-academia project to demonstrate power generation in the 2030s. Conceptual design completed in one year (November 2024 → November 2025); **Japan's first private-sector-led conceptual design report**. Design: **~50 MW fusion output from a JT-60SA-class device using NBI heating**, combining JT-60SA's proven technology with Japanese manufacturing. Seeking funding in 2026 for engineering design.
- **Kyoto Fusioneering** ($144 m) — notable for *not* building a reactor. It supplies gyrotrons, tritium systems and blanket technology to everyone else, and is building **UNITY-1** (Japan) and **UNITY-2** (Canada). It is relocating its US HQ to Oak Ridge, Tennessee.
- **[[ex-fusion|EX-Fusion]]** ($38 m) — DPSSL direct-drive laser fusion.
- Japanese corporate investors are unusually prominent in the FIA's investor list — Mitsubishi, Mitsui, Sumitomo Mitsui, JERA, Kansai Electric, NTT, KDDI, Toyota Ventures, Development Bank of Japan, Mizuho, Daiwa.

Japan's distinguishing feature is that its **large industrial conglomerates are investing directly**, which is the classic Japanese pattern for commercialising a technology and is arguably a stronger signal than venture capital.

## South Korea

- **KSTAR** (KFE, Daejeon) — the first tokamak built entirely with Nb₃Sn superconductors. Long-pulse and high-temperature research; ion temperatures above 100 million K for tens of seconds; **tungsten divertor installed 2023–24**, making it directly ITER-relevant. A major site for AI plasma-control research with Princeton and DeepMind.
- **K-DEMO** — the planned demonstration reactor, developed with US collaboration.
- **Basic Plan for Fusion Energy Development Promotion** — connected to the **"K-Moonshot Project"**, accelerating the timeline for fusion power demonstration. Korea has framed fusion commercialisation as a 2030s–2040s objective integrating AI, superconductivity and advanced materials.
- Korea is an ITER Member (9.1% share) and manufactures vacuum vessel sectors and other major components.

## India

- **Institute for Plasma Research (IPR)**, Gandhinagar — the national centre. Operates **[[aditya-u|ADITYA-U]]** and **SST-1** (Steady State Superconducting Tokamak).
- **ITER-India** — India joined ITER in 2005 and supplies the cryostat (the largest single ITER component), cryolines, and heating systems.
- **Three private companies** now appear in the FIA survey — the fastest-growing national cluster by count:
  - **ASPL Fusion** (Gandhinagar, $2 m) — incubated at IPR/DAE. A phased magnetic-mirror roadmap: BNCT clinical facility → HTS neutron source for isotopes → **sub-critical fusion-fission hybrid** (35 MW) → 250 MW pure fusion plant, target 2041. Notable for planning near-term revenue from boron neutron capture therapy and neutron activation analysis.
  - **Pranos Fusion** ($7.2 m)
  - **Anubal Fusion** (Hyderabad, $0.5 m) — petawatt laser, p-¹¹B
- The FIA notes India's emergence "reflects a need to serve the energy demand and technological growth of the world's most populous country."

## Russia

- **T-15MD** (Kurchatov Institute) — test-launched May 2021; first high-temperature plasma March 2023. The immediate goal is achieving design parameters.
- **[[globus-m2|Globus-M2]]** (Ioffe Institute) — spherical tokamak.
- **T-11M** — plasma-facing materials research, including lithium walls.
- **TRT** (Tokamak with Reactor Technologies), at **TRINITI**, Troitsk — the flagship next-generation device and intended prototype for a commercial plant. **Preliminary design completed.**
- Russia remains an ITER Member, manufacturing and supplying **25 distinct high-technology systems** — a collaboration that has notably survived the geopolitical rupture since 2022, and one of very few Russia-West scientific programmes that has.
- Russia is also, with China, one of the two historic sources of **enriched [[lithium-6]]**, which makes it a chokepoint in the tritium breeding supply chain for everybody else.

## Cross-cutting: the geopolitics

The FIA's 2026 report frames the competition candidly: *"the first nations to commercialize fusion could gain profound leverage over global energy markets and an early push to develop the global supply chains and jobs that follow."*

**The chokepoints that actually matter:**

| Input | Concentration | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| **REBCO / HTS tape** | Global capacity ~10,000 km/yr; China holds significant manufacturing strength | Every high-field magnetic concept depends on it |
| **Enriched lithium-6** | Historically Russia and China | Every D-T plant needs it for breeding blankets |
| **Tritium** | CANDU reactors (Canada, Korea, Romania) | 30–40 kg global inventory; start-up inventory for a fleet is unobtainable |
| **Beryllium** | Narrow supply base | Neutron multiplication in many blanket designs |
| **Tungsten** | China dominant | Plasma-facing components |

The US response has been [[arpa-e|ARPA-E]] SCALEUP funding for domestic HTS magnet lines (**$20 m to Thea Energy**, July 2026), CFS commercialising magnets as a merchant product, and the DOE roadmap making "Seed Fusion Supply Chains" an explicit Grow-pillar action. The UK's LIBRTI addresses lithium and tritium directly. Neither is yet at scale.

**The scientific-openness question.** Fusion has been unusually open since the 1958 Geneva declassification, and that openness materially accelerated the field. The 2026 picture is more mixed: Chinese companies declining to report to the FIA, HTS tape becoming an export-control question, and national strategies framed around leadership rather than collaboration. ITER remains the counterexample — 35 nations including Russia, still cooperating. Whether the ITER model or the competitive model dominates the next decade is genuinely unsettled, and it will affect how fast the field moves.
