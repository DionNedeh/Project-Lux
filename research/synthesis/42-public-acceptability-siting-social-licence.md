# 42 — Public Acceptability, Siting and Social Licence

*Project Lux · Why favourable polling is a starting condition, not permission to build*

---

## 1. Fusion’s support is real and fragile

Fusion is commonly presented as the nuclear technology the public will accept: no self-sustaining chain reaction, less long-lived waste, abundant fuel and a future clean-energy role. Survey evidence does often find favourable responses. The mistake is turning that response into a durable social licence for a specific facility.

The 2026 [[public-acceptability-scoping-review-2026|scoping review]] found only **14 eligible research articles from 2005–2025**, concentrated in Europe and the United States. It characterises attitudes as broadly positive but tentative and weakly held. The six-country [[fusion-fission-twin-conjoint-public-support-2026|twin conjoint experiment]] found unconditional support of **73.5% for fusion and 71.5% for next-generation fission**. That is a slight fusion advantage, not a categorical escape from nuclear politics.

Public acceptability asks whether people judge a technology broadly acceptable. Siting acceptance asks whether they tolerate a project in a place. Social licence is the continuing legitimacy that lets institutions build and operate through disruption, disagreement and change. Polling measures the first imperfectly and cannot grant the latter two.

## 2. Why the evidence base is thinner than the confidence

Fourteen studies over two decades are not enough to support universal claims. Fusion opinion research is geographically narrow, often cross-sectional and conducted before respondents face a real planning application. Awareness is low, so answers can be sensitive to question wording and the information immediately supplied.

That does not make the evidence meaningless. It changes what it can establish. A survey can identify associations among affect, prior fission attitudes, perceived risks and benefits, institutional trust and stated support. It cannot show that an attitude will survive a cost overrun, contested environmental assessment, construction traffic or an incident elsewhere.

The review’s strongest recurring variables are prior fission attitudes, affective response, risk–benefit perception and trust. Fusion communication does not begin on an empty page. People map a new technology onto institutions and histories they already know: nuclear power, government competence, energy bills, industrial employment and fairness between regions.

The evidence also challenges the idea that more facts mechanically produce consent. Information can move attitudes, but the effect depends on detail, framing and source. A message that lists advantages while hiding uncertainty may produce an immediate favourable answer and a later trust penalty when omitted trade-offs emerge.

## 3. Project attributes compete with the technology label

The conjoint experiment, fielded in **May 2025** in France, Germany, Italy, the United Kingdom, Spain and Poland, varies attributes of hypothetical fusion and advanced-fission projects. Respondents weigh distance from homes, plant size, local employment, fuel origin and electricity-price effects rather than answering only whether fusion sounds good.

The results show that governance choices matter. Lower prices were especially attractive. Local jobs, domestic or European fuel sourcing, distance and size also shifted preferences. Across six packages, **60% of respondents rejected none** and **3.8% rejected all**, according to Civera and colleagues. Most people were therefore neither automatic opponents nor unconditional supporters.

These are stated choices in a survey experiment, not observations of an actual siting conflict. Their value is analytical: they show that the word “fusion” does not dominate the package. A developer can lose an initially favourable audience through high expected bills, distant supply-chain benefits, imposed site choice or unclear fuel governance.

Conversely, attributes are not public-relations levers to manipulate. If local jobs exist only in a slide deck, or low prices require an unsupported [[lcoe-fusion|LCOE]] assumption, the offer is not a social-licence strategy. It is a claim that will later be tested.

## 4. Siting changes the question

National respondents consider an abstract technology. Host communities consider land, water, roads, landscape, tax base, property, emergency arrangements and who has decision authority. Even a plant with low routine emissions occupies a place and connects to infrastructure.

Fusion sites may need high-capacity grid connections, cooling systems, secure tritium handling, component transport and substantial construction work. A retired coal or nuclear site can offer grid access, industrial land and an experienced workforce. It can also carry histories of pollution, closure or imposed national decisions. “Brownfield” is not synonymous with “socially available”.

Distance effects in the conjoint study should therefore be read as evidence of place sensitivity, not dismissed as a knowledge deficit. People living near a project bear concentrated disruption while national climate and innovation benefits are diffuse. A fair process makes that distribution explicit and gives local participants influence over mitigations and benefits.

## 5. Trust is produced by institutions under pressure

[[27-people-institutions-public-trust|Document 27]] argues that public trust is infrastructure. The social-science evidence sharpens that claim: trust is not a communications asset stored before controversy. It is a judgement about whether institutions disclose uncertainty, keep commitments, correct errors and share power when incentives favour concealment.

Three moments are especially diagnostic:

1. **Before site selection:** Are criteria published, alternatives compared and exclusions explained?
2. **During permission and construction:** Can communities see evidence, challenge assumptions and obtain independent expertise?
3. **After an adverse event or schedule failure:** Does the operator disclose promptly, investigate independently and revise commitments?

A glossy visitor centre cannot compensate for a closed design process. Nor does participation mean that every participant receives a veto. It means influence is real enough to alter site layout, transport routes, monitoring, benefits, emergency plans or, where evidence warrants, the site decision itself.

Independent institutions matter because developers and sponsoring governments have delivery incentives. Community access to technical assistance, public monitoring data and grievance mechanisms can make disagreement more informed and reduce dependence on company interpretation.

## 6. Information must preserve the evidence hierarchy

Fusion communication fails when it merges demonstrated physics, designed plants and announced schedules. A responsible public account should say:

- what a machine has demonstrated and for how long;
- whether a quoted gain is (Q_{sci}), (Q_{target}), (Q_{wp}) or (Q_{eng});
- which systems remain designed rather than integrated;
- what waste and tritium inventories a particular design expects;
- what cost and schedule assumptions remain projections;
- which regulator has authority and which rules are unfinished.

This is not a case for leading with every technical caveat. It is a case for making the boundary between fact and ambition stable across documents, meetings and setbacks. [[39-the-discourse|Document 39]] shows why: fusion’s long history of moving timelines makes unsupported certainty especially expensive.

Comparisons with fission should also be specific. Fusion lacks a fission chain reaction and has a different accident envelope. It can still produce activated material and, for deuterium–tritium systems, manage radioactive tritium. “No nuclear waste” is misleading; “the waste profile can be different and potentially shorter-lived, depending on materials and operation” is testable.

## 7. Benefits must be governed, not promised

Local employment appears in the conjoint evidence because it changes who benefits. A social-licence programme should translate that preference into enforceable mechanisms: apprenticeship numbers, procurement targets, transparent eligibility, wage standards and reporting. It should distinguish temporary construction work from permanent operating jobs.

Community benefits can include tax revenue, infrastructure, local ownership, bill support, research partnerships or transition funds. Their legitimacy depends on timing and process. Benefits disclosed only after opposition appears can look like payment for silence. Benefits co-designed before commitment can reflect actual local priorities.

Electricity price deserves similar honesty. The survey finds that price matters; it does not prove fusion will deliver cheap electricity. [[10-economics-and-markets|Document 10]] shows that capital cost, availability, component replacement and financing dominate future economics. A developer can discuss target costs and uncertainty, but should not present an unbuilt plant’s tariff as demonstrated.

Fuel-source preferences point to governance rather than chemistry alone. Respondents may value domestic or European sourcing because it signals security, oversight and shared economic benefit. For fusion, a credible explanation must cover initial tritium supply, breeding assumptions, accountancy and international dependencies—not simply repeat that deuterium is abundant.

## 8. A staged social-licence process

An implementable process can match project maturity:

**Exploration.** Publish siting criteria, map plausible regions and commission baseline opinion and stakeholder research before selecting a favourite. Include organised opponents, workers, local government, emergency services, marginalised residents and people who do not attend formal meetings.

**Shortlisting.** Compare sites using the same technical, environmental and social criteria. Fund independent community advice. Record which concerns change the ranking and which do not, with reasons.

**Co-design.** Negotiate transport, water, landscape, monitoring, workforce and benefit arrangements. Put measurable commitments into agreements rather than promotional prose.

**Permission.** Present a traceable safety and environmental case. Make uncertainty and dissent visible. Avoid treating statutory consultation as proof of consent.

**Construction and operation.** Maintain public dashboards, incident reporting, grievance resolution and periodic review. Social licence can be withdrawn; it is not earned once at groundbreaking.

**Closure.** Establish decommissioning funding, site end state and workforce transition before operation. Communities should not inherit an unfunded obligation if the company or programme fails.

## 9. Measurement should follow behaviour, not only sentiment

Approve/disapprove polling is useful but insufficient. A serious programme should track:

- awareness and confidence separately from support;
- trust in each responsible institution, not “the sector” in aggregate;
- perceived distribution of benefits and burdens;
- participation diversity and whether input changed decisions;
- complaint resolution time and repeated grievance themes;
- local procurement, training and permanent-job delivery;
- changes after milestones, delays, incidents and cost disclosures.

Longitudinal panels around real projects would materially improve the field. They could show when initial support stabilises, polarises or collapses, and which institutional actions predict recovery. Comparative studies outside Europe and the United States are equally important. The current evidence base cannot be assumed to represent communities in Asia, Africa, Latin America or the Middle East.

## 10. What is defensible now

As of 26 August 2026, it is defensible to say that fusion often receives favourable stated support, including in large European studies, and that a six-country experiment found 73.5% unconditional support. It is equally necessary to say that the research base is small, awareness is low, preferences are weakly held, and project attributes materially change responses.

No existing survey grants a social licence to [[step|STEP]], [[sparc|SPARC]], [[iter|ITER]] or any private plant. Each site will encounter its own institutional history and distribution of costs and benefits. The fusion label is an opening advantage, not a durable mandate.

The best strategy is therefore not persuasion after a site is chosen. It is governance before commitment: publish criteria, preserve uncertainty, let participation alter decisions, make benefits enforceable and maintain independent scrutiny through operation and closure. If fusion becomes commercially viable, social legitimacy will not be a soft issue outside engineering. It will be one of the systems that determines whether plants can be built repeatedly.
